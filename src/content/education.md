We wanted to pass on what we were learning, from our own lab work and from our
stakeholder research, to people outside the project. That took three forms:
street interviews with the public, accessible material on social media, and a
podcast that beekeepers and the general public can use to educate themselves on
honey bee health.

The bar we set for this page is whether anything went **in both directions**:
whether we changed how someone thought about synthetic biology, and whether
they changed anything about how we work. We cannot currently clear that bar,
because we did not record the activities as we ran them.

**Undocumented outreach scores nothing, and reconstructing attendance after the
fact is not acceptable.** So this page lists what genuinely happened, marks what
is unrecorded, and says what is planned. It will be rewritten as the record is
recovered and the planned work lands.

### Buzz In, the podcast

Our podcast, **Buzz In**, was designed as a platform to integrate stakeholders'
perspectives. It brought together leading experts in honey bee research with
commercial beekeepers, to tackle honey bee health and consider the future of the
*Varroa* mite problem.

The reason it exists is on the human practices page: Professor Paul Lam set out
risk handling as identify, assess, manage and communicate, and singled out
communication with beekeepers and the public as **the hardest and most important
step**. The brief that follows from that is a podcast which discusses the risks
of an engineered treatment as openly as the benefits, rather than one that
advertises the project.

#### Episode 1 — "To treat or not to treat: Are chemical treatments the future of tackling Varroa mites?"

We hosted **Professor Stephen Martin** and **Paul Horton**, a commercial
beekeeper based in Lincolnshire. They disagreed about whether a world in which
treatments are not needed is achievable; Paul Horton felt it was unrealistic.

## A glossary of bee terms

A plain-language glossary of the terms used across this wiki, for someone with no
background in it. Ten beekeeping terms are written up, in the bee lab's own
words; the molecular terms are not, and the gap is recorded at the end.

**Frame** — A removable wooden or plastic panel that fits inside a hive, holding
a sheet of wax or plastic foundation on which bees build hexagonal cells. Frames
are the working unit beekeepers and researchers pull out of a hive to inspect
brood or honey stores, or, in this project, to locate larvae at specific
developmental stages and to introduce mites. A single frame can be removed,
worked on outside the hive, and returned without disturbing the rest of the
colony.

**Haemolymph** — The fluid equivalent of blood in insects, circulating through an
open body cavity rather than closed vessels. It carries nutrients, immune cells
and, in this project, ingested dsRNA around the body. It is extracted from bees
and larvae to measure how much dsRNA has been absorbed and how long it persists
before degrading.

**Larvae** — The legless, grub-like juvenile stage of a honeybee, hatching from
an egg and developing through several growth stages before pupating. Larvae are
fed continuously by nurse bees and are the life stage most vulnerable to *Varroa*
feeding once a cell is capped. They are a key point in the pathway this project
is characterising, since *Varroa* enter the cell to feed and reproduce during
this stage.

**Instar** — A distinct growth stage of a larva, marked out by moulting;
honeybee larvae pass through five instars before pupating. Later instars,
particularly the 5th, are larger, more developed and closer to capping, which
makes them easier to identify and handle for dosing or dissection. Instar matters
experimentally because larval sensitivity, haemolymph volume and proximity to
*Varroa* infestation all change across these stages.

**Adult bees** — Fully developed, winged honeybees that have emerged from their
pupal cell. Adult bees take on different roles depending on age, including nurse
bees (feeding larvae) and forager bees (collecting nectar and pollen outside the
hive). This project worked primarily with newly emerged adults, whose behaviour
and physiology most closely relate to the nurse-bee stage of the pathway being
characterised.

**Capping / capped cell** — The wax seal nurse bees place over a cell once a
larva reaches a certain developmental point, sealing it off to pupate
undisturbed. *Varroa* mites specifically time their entry to just before capping,
so that they become sealed inside with the developing bee to feed and reproduce.
Capping marks the transition separating pre-capping larvae, which are accessible
and dosable, from post-capping individuals, which must be uncapped to reach.

**Pupa** — The developmental stage between larva and adult, during which the
insect is largely immobile and undergoes metamorphosis inside a capped cell.
Pupae were used in several assays as a feeding host for mites, since *Varroa*
naturally feed on and reproduce alongside a bee at this stage.

**Nurse bee** — A young adult worker bee whose role within the colony is to feed
and care for larvae, including producing the glandular secretions (jelly) that
make up larval food. Nurse bees are the biological link this project is trying to
characterise, since they are the route by which ingested dsRNA could plausibly
reach larvae.

**Forager bee** — An older adult worker bee whose role is to leave the hive to
collect nectar, pollen or water. Foragers were used in an early feeding pilot but
were found harder to handle and restrain than newly emerged bees, which
contributed to the decision to use newly emerged, nurse-stage bees for later
assays — see [results](/results).

**Colony / hive** — A colony is the full social unit of bees, queen, workers and
drones, living and working together; a hive is the physical structure housing
them. Frames, brood and stores all sit within the hive, and colony-level
decisions, such as which hives had enough *Varroa* or enough newly emerged bees,
shaped which hives were used for which assay throughout this project.
