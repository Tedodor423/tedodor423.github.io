## Across both regions

The same technology meets very different realities. A treatment that is
affordable in one beekeeping economy is not in another; a regulatory route that
is open in one country is closed in the next; and the pest pressure itself
differs enough to change what a useful treatment would even look like.

Rejecting the idea of a single universal deployment context is one of the
conclusions of [our human practices work](/human-practices), and this page is
where that conclusion is worked out.

## Where the losses are

Run the years and the picture is not one epidemic spreading outward. It is a
band of countries reporting steady losses, one country pulling away from the
rest, and a continent that only starts counting in 2023.

Three things in that figure set up the case studies. The United States climbs
from **36% of colonies lost to 55.6%** over the run. Australia has three years of
data and no more, because the mite only arrived in 2022. The United Kingdom has
**a single measured year**, and every other year drawn for it is an inference from
the European average.

## The regions, and what each one is for

- **[Australia](/case-studies/australia)** is the recent-arrival case: four
  years from detection to endemic, resistance already established, and an
  industry visibly deciding whether to stay in business. It tells us what a
  treatment must survive — climate, no brood break, reinfestation from feral
  colonies — and it is the only place that gave us **a binary regulatory answer** on
  the chassis.
- **[California](/case-studies/california)** is the scale case: about 80% of US
  commercial colonies converging on one bloom, 700 colonies per worker, and a
  pollination contract that makes **a dead colony cost twice**. It tells us what a
  treatment must not demand — extra visits, complicated administration, anything
  that does not exist at commercial volume.

## How each case study is written

Seven parts, the same order every time, so the two regions can be read against
each other: **pest situation · current practice · costs and constraints ·
regulation · stakeholder attitudes · what an acceptable treatment would need to
do · implication for NECTAR.**

## What differs between regions

| Axis                     | Australia                                                                      | California                                                                     |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pest pressure            | Detected 2022, endemic, spread held to three eastern states; feral collapse seeded managed hives | Endemic for decades; resistance a running battle since 1997                    |
| Scale of operation       | Commercial operations of 1,000+ hives; small 200–300 hive operators being bought out | 8,500-colony operations, 5,000 sent to almonds; about 700 colonies per worker  |
| Treatment economics      | Roughly 60% product / 40% labour; formic acid about A$7 per hive                | Treatment products alone 9% of total costs, before labour and colony replacement |
| Regulatory route         | Non-living yeast outside the OGTR's remit; both *S. alvi* versions inside it    | Multi-agency and function-based; possibly an animal drug rather than a pesticide |
| Attitudes to an engineered input | Consumers expected to oppose anything GM; beekeepers nonetheless preferred living *S. alvi* on labour | Judged on scale and cost; a living GMO expected to face a longer route to market |

The two columns **disagree about the same product**, which is the finding. Australia
rules out the living chassis on regulation and consumer acceptance while its
beekeepers want it; California is indifferent to the chassis and unforgiving
about labour. A design that satisfies both is the one we built: non-living, and
delivered inside **a job the beekeeper already does**.
