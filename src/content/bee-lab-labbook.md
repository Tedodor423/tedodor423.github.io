## The dated record

### June 2026

#### 29 June — Anree, Sammi, Theo, Freddie, Diogo

Cleaned the lab. Prepared 1 M sucrose solutions at three NaCl concentrations,
3 × 175 ml each: 0.5145 g → 50 mM, 1.0215 g → 100 mM, 1.5311 g → 150 mM, plus
about 475 ml of plain sucrose. Logged the reference points for the coming salt
assay: bees preferentially consume 0.1–0.3% salt `[LIT]`, and dsRNA stability is
reportedly best around 100–150 mM `[LIT]`.

#### 30 June — Anree, Sammi, Theo, Freddie, Diogo, Ari, Michael

Set up 15 nutrition assay boxes, 4 feeding tubes each: 6 × plain 1 M sucrose,
3 × +50 mM NaCl, 3 × +100 mM, 3 × +150 mM. Boxes needed the fourth hole taped
over; only three of four were factory-drilled. The assay write-up splits the six
plain boxes further: **five with bees, and one with no bees** as the evaporation
control, which is the split the statistics on [the bee lab](/bee-lab) use.

Air bubbles in filled tubes are very hard to correct once formed. Empty and
refill from scratch instead.

Standing procedure agreed: make solutions same-day where possible, since fridge
storage causes evaporation; count and log dead bees at every solution change so
daily sucrose usage can be corrected.

Decision meeting with Anjum on larval rearing. **In-house rearing ruled out**: two
to three months of training, "a paper in itself" at York. Alternative agreed:
apply dsRNA directly to frame cells during the natural nurse-bee feeding window,
days 4–7 of larval life, avoiding the sensitive first four days, then check
survival at day 10. Mites enter cells about two days pre-pupation, so dsRNA
persistence timing governs dosing. **Whole-hive feeding ruled out** as too risky to
colony and queen health; localised single-frame dosing with three colony
replicates adopted instead.

### July 2026

#### 1 July — Freddie, Michael

Continued the sucrose feeding assay; weighed fresh solutions, replaced day-old
tubes. Dead-bee counts logged per tube at replacement (tube 9: 2 dead; tube 13:
1 dead) to correct consumption per bee. Learned that forcing a feeding tube into
a crowded cage causes major spillage; the fix is to discard and use a fresh cage.

#### 2 July — Freddie, Michael, Ari, Roman

Filled new feeding tubes: 6 × 1 M sucrose, 1 × evaporation control, 3 × 50 mM,
3 × 100 mM, 3 × 150 mM. Standing rule set: used feeding tubes are hand-washed
outside the lab and returned to a labelled bucket, not general waste.

#### 3–4 July — Anree, Freddie, Roman, Ari, Diogo, Theo, Michael

Finished the sucrose feeding assay. Practised gut dissection (ileum
visualisation; gut extracted by pulling the stinger). Extracted haemolymph from
the feeding-experiment bees by the centrifugal protocol above and stored at
−80 °C for future spiking experiments.

#### 6 July — Anree, Freddie, Ari, Diogo, Theo

Dan demonstrated the larval developmental stages; the team visited the hive to
identify eggs and instars. Planning: inducer toxicity assay, fat body
dissection, larval dissection flagged as next steps.

#### 7 July — Freddie, Sammi, Anree, Diogo, Ari

Anjum demonstrated identifying 5th-instar larvae: 7–8 mm, curved, easiest to
find near already-capped cells because the egg-laying pattern creates an age
gradient across the frame. Noted that larval haemolymph extraction needs
delicacy; pinching removes the haemolymph instantly, so specialised tools or
very careful tweezer work are needed. Established a gut-dissection and
haemolymph protocol for the following Wednesday.

#### 8 July — Michael, Theo, Anree, Freddie, Sammi

Collected one frame with enough larvae past the 4th instar, under instruction
not to disturb capped brood. Tweezers were most efficient for removal, with a
puncture risk from pointed ones. Established the larval haemolymph method and
the adult dissection steps recorded in the protocols above.

#### 10 July — Anree, Theo, Freddie

Haemolymph extraction from bee larvae.

#### 10 July (second group) — Diogo, Michael

Built smaller feeding tubes: Eppendorf tubes cut just below the 1.5 ml mark, a
hole in the top for a PCR tube to pass through, and two small side holes in the
PCR tube matching the standard feeding-tube hole size.

#### 11 July

3D printing discussed; printer precision about 0.05 mm. Considered a
translucent, autoclave-safe resin (about £150) for reusable feeding tubes, held
off pending the first sucrose trial results.

#### 13 July

Sourced fine-gauge needles for hole-poking. Jeri confirmed to run Friday's
session in Anna's absence.

#### 14 July

3D-printed PCR-tube adapters arrived, some with print defects but usable.

PER assay attempted with more bees: bees repeatedly slipped out of the gorilla
tape restraints, were difficult to feed, and several redos were needed. A
separate enclosure trial with 23 mixed brood and adult bees took very long, and
some bees may have gone unfed overnight. Bees began escaping enclosures during
setup, flagged as a technique to improve.

Set up a PCR feeding-tube evaporation assay: 6 adapter combinations × 2 repeats
× 4 tubes, tracked in a shared spreadsheet. The assay write-up gives **five**
adaptor combinations for the same comparison, at 250 µl of 1 M sucrose in
deionised water per PCR tube; the two counts need reconciling.

#### 15 July

Discussed refining the PER restraint with a strap over the abdomen. Set the plan
for the following week's large-scale PER assay: 400 bees in 8 batches of about
50, four treatments (300, 500 and 700 bp with Mango; 500 bp without), 5
concentrations, 5 replicates, 2 timepoints per batch. Flagged the need for 4+
people per session, for low-dsRNA-binding storage tubes, and for a stability
check of dsRNA held in 1 M sucrose for about 3 h before feeding.

#### 16 July

Practice day: fed water to bees, practised catching and harnessing, timed
haemolymph-extraction throughput. Dedicated extraction tubes could not be found
in the lab, so PCR tubes inside Eppendorf tubes were improvised; the centrifuge
itself turned out to be contaminated and needed cleaning. Flagged an order of
about 450 proper centrifuge tubes. Standing instruction reiterated: keep the
journal updated daily, including bee-handling experience.

#### 17–18 July

Risk assessment for dsRNA work drafted and sent to Hafez for signature. Feeding
concentrations under review to reduce the number of bees needed. Throughput
benchmarks: 20 bees strapped and fed in 1 h by one person; 50 bees in 2 h by two
people, expected to be faster with newly-emerged than with forager bees.

#### 20 July

Single 700 bp dosing session, doubling as practice for Freddie. Weekend

#### 21 July

Batch of 66 3D-printed adapters completed, about 1.5–2 h print time.

#### 22 July

**First dsRNA feeding pilot** on adult bees: 20 bees strapped, PER-fed and
haemolymph-extracted, 10 at a 1 µg dose and 10 at 200 ng.

Larval feeding pilot: 15 instar-5 larvae fed, 5 at 1 µg and 10 at 200 ng, marked
on the frame with red and black lines respectively. Technique noted: 5 µl
applied by micropipette as a dabbed droplet, without the tip touching the larva.

Workflow gap logged: tighter tracking needed of which bee was fed when against

#### 27 July

#### 28 July

Second larval spiking pilot: 10 instar-5 larvae spiked with 5 µl of 200 µg/µl
dsRNA. Larvae 2 and 6 were double-dosed after the first dose missed the cell. A

#### 29 July

Haemolymph extracted from the 10 larvae spiked on 28 July, mixing in-frame and
out-of-frame technique. Typical yield about 10 µl per larva; tube 3 gave 5 µl,
tube 6 was contaminated by submersion in brood food, tube 8 gave 8 µl, tube 10
gave 11 µl. Flash-frozen on dry ice and transported to DCHB. Same day: a qPCR
run, then a gel once the station was free.

#### 30 July

Third larval feeding pilot. Made a 5× diluted stock: 40 µl of 200 µg/µl dsRNA
plus 160 µl sucrose. Spiked 12 larvae across 4th and 5th instar, marked by
coloured lines and position on the frame for re-identification. Spiking at 12:45.

#### 31 July

Extraction of the third pilot, organised into 6th-instar (1–8) and 5th-instar
(1–4) groups; two 5 µl draws per larva attempted to reach 10 µl. Yields
inconsistent — 6th-instar-8 gave about 6 µl, 5th-instar-5 gave about 0 µl.
Flash-freezing of two 5th instars was imperfect: punctured before tube transfer,
though added rapidly.

**Key finding.** 5th-instar larvae can be extracted without breaking.
6th-instar larvae are too soft. Later pupal stages are firmer still and have
lost the osmotic pressure that lets haemolymph ooze out, so they would need an
adult-style extraction.

Two flash-frozen 5th-instar samples at the 1 µg dose taken forward for RNA
extraction and RT-PCR.

### September 2026

#### 1 September

Mite-tray check across all hives to assess Varroa numbers before a collection
decision. Most promising: Hive 40 (100+), Hive 20 (75+), Hive 14 (75+), Hive 43
(50+). Many hives had very low counts. Plan to repeat the count over two days.

#### 2 September

Repeated mite-tray counts across all hives; full table logged. **Sugar shake on
Hive 26 failed**, attributed to rain making the sugar clump and stick to bees
and frames rather than shake off. Retry planned once dry.

#### 3 September

Third day of mite-tray counts. Successful sugar shake on Hive 14 in dry
conditions, yielding plentiful mites — about 60 collected that day, with fine
paintbrushes sourced for picking them up. Mites washed in 0.9% NaCl in a petri
dish, then transferred to 30 µl of 0.9% NaCl in Eppendorf tubes, 10 mites per
tube, to soak 6 hours. Post-soak, split into three incubator groups: soaked
mites on pupae in gelatin capsules; mites in capsules without pupae; unsoaked
mites in Eppendorf tubes.

#### 4 September

Group 1 (soaked, on pupae, 3 mites per pupa): average 35.7% survival across 10
replicates, with some mites falling off the pupae, which may confound the
reading. Group 2 (capsule, no pupa): 0/3, 0/3, 1/3 across 3 replicates. Group 3
(unsoaked, Eppendorf tubes alone): none survived; note logged to collect these
fresh from the sugar shake next time rather than reusing leftovers from the
count. Recommendation logged that **pre-soak washing should be avoided** or
minimised in volume, as it appeared to increase mortality.

#### 6 September

Week plan: meet Anjum to redesign the pupae/mite assay; scrap the 0.9%
NaCl-only test and soak mites directly in 20 µl of 1 µg dsGFP solution instead;
redo the gelatin-capsule larval-mite assay with more careful handling, 10 larvae
and 10 mites to match the first pilot's scale; and add an in-frame
mite-insertion experiment, 10–20 larvae, one mite per cell, same dsGFP soak.
Lisa and Anna to be trained given the upcoming full mite assay and possible
repeats of the adult and larval stability assays, both targeted for completion
within two weeks because the brood season was closing.

#### 7 September

Cellulose capsules arrived. Second mite-housing pilot: 40 mites soaked in 0.9%
NaCl for 6 h, then split — 10 soaked into 6th/7th-instar capped pupae in-frame;
4 unsoaked into capped pupae in-frame; 5 soaked onto 5th-instar larvae in
cellulose capsules; 5 soaked onto white-eyed pupae; 9 unsoaked alone in
capsules; 9 soaked alone in capsules. Into the incubator at 20:20.

Decision: hold the capped-cell groups **until day 3** rather than checking early.
Disturbing capped cells risks losing mites, which crawl to the sides and bottom,
and reliable survival assessment needs a minimum three-day window.

#### 8 September

Baseline reported: **40% mite survival inside cells** at 24 h. Three-day checkpoint:
4/10 survived in-frame cells; 3/5 on white-eyed pupae in capsules; 2/5 on larvae
in capsules; 0/9 soaked alone; 0/9 unsoaked alone.

Team consensus: a larger sample size is needed whichever method wins, and the
pilot may be worth repeating with bigger capsule groups. Plan for the next day:
compare in-frame against capsule directly, drop the unsoaked-alone control, aim
for 20–30 mites per group, and check mites are alive before placement. Lisa and
Anna to be trained on adult haemolymph extraction and PER strapping.

#### 9 September

Nearly finished adding Varroa to cells. Most mites survived soaking this round.
Standing recommendation recorded: always soak three to four times the number of
mites actually needed, to buffer losses.

#### 10 September

qPCR primer design for mite targets. Gel-type clarification logged for two prior
gels: small grey gels are 1% agarose/TAE, the large white tray is 1.5%
agarose/TBE.

#### 11 September

dsRNA moved to −80 °C, approximate concentration known pending a Qubit
re-check. RNase-free tips depleted; a plate left open overnight flagged. Sugar
shake performed on half the bees in a hive, documented on video and photo.
Ten mites each added to plain water and to low- and high-concentration target
dsRNA (about 120 mites in water, about 70 at each concentration); incubated
6 hours.

Late-night debrief flagged four findings.

1. The Qubit-validated dsRNA concentration came back **far below the intended
   1000 ng/µl** soaking dose. Recommendation to log raw Nanodrop, unprocessed
   Qubit, post-DNase/RNase-I Nanodrop and processed Qubit for every batch — a
   comparison nobody appears to have published for Varroa dsRNA work.
2. Mites took over five hours to wake after the soak, making a large single-day
   screen impractical. Retrospective note that freezing woken mites rather than
   discarding them would have been better practice.
3. **Mite mortality during the soak was very high: 140 controls soaked, 40
   alive afterwards.**
4. New assay design proposed, from a suggestion by Anjum adapted by the team:
   spike larvae as in the 5th-instar pilot, confirm signal on a gel, leave the
   dosed larvae in-hive for a day to reach capping, then remove the frame and
   introduce live mites fresh from a sugar shake by carefully uncapping and
   resealing cells. Flagged as needing much more care than earlier attempts,
   since nurse bees remove visibly damaged cells and pupae if the frame is out
   too long. It avoids the soak-and-wait bottleneck, reduces drowning losses and
   would yield a dose–response between larval dsRNA dose and mite mortality,
   contingent on producing more target dsRNA by IVT.
