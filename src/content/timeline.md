## Month by month

The same record as the chart, in order, with one line per milestone.

### February 2026

The team's working channels open on **12 February**, and the project-selection
phase begins: seven ideas pitched, cut to three.

### March 2026

**29 March** — the supervisor rejects the project the team had voted for, on five
objections, none of which is that the science is wrong. See
[Description](/project-description).

### April 2026

The replacement project is chosen by a team vote, and **NECTAR becomes the
project**: RNA interference against _Varroa destructor_, delivered through the
hive's own feeding behaviour.

### June 2026

- **18 June** — a mite count finds almost none in the frames. Collecting enough
  _Varroa_ before August is impossible, so **the plan inverts**: dose bees first,
  use the results to cut the target list, reach the mites later.
- **29–30 June** — the bee lab opens. Fifteen nutrition-assay boxes are set up on
  1 M sucrose with three NaCl concentrations, and a feasibility meeting rules out
  in-house larval rearing (two to three months of training) in favour of applying
  dsRNA directly to frame cells during the day 4–7 nurse-feeding window.

### July 2026

- **3–8 July** — haemolymph extraction is established at both scales: a
  centrifugal method for adults, and for larvae a ventral incision below the head
  segment with an entomological pin. Larval staging is taught on the frame.
- **10–21 July** — the feeder iterates three times. Large tubes waste too much
  dsRNA in dead volume, so they are cut down and fitted with PCR tubes, then with
  3D-printed adaptors; 66 adaptors are printed by 21 July. Four tube orientations
  are compared on spillage and evaporation.
- **14–15 July** — the proboscis-extension assay fails on foragers, which slip
  their restraints and do not survive the day. The full assay is planned at 400
  bees in 8 batches, and newly emerged bees become the standard animal.
- **20 July** — a weekend survivability check gives 0/15, 1/16 and 2/15 deaths,
  establishing low background mortality. The same week, dsRNA will not anneal:
  there is **no salt in the reaction**.
- **22 July** — the first dsRNA feeding pilots, adults and larvae, at 1 µg and
  200 ng.
- **23 July** — an interview with a honey company's science and regulatory leads
  retires the engineered gut symbiont and **makes yeast the lead platform**. See
  [cycle 2.2](/engineering#cycle-2-2).
- **28–31 July** — three larval spiking pilots. 5th-instar larvae can be extracted
  without breaking; 6th instars are too soft. Five RNA extraction methods are
  compared on the same larvae (**29 July**) and give the same yield.
- **30–31 July** — the first qPCR standard curve, and then a 100 bp band appears
  in a water control that should be empty.

### August 2026

- **1–11 August** — the contamination diagnosis runs to five rounds and ends in a
  2×2×2 factorial. Only new water, new primers and a different polymerase come
  out clean. See [cycle 3.3b](/engineering#cycle-3-3b).
- **4–5 August** — the first real result. Whole larvae and larval haemolymph give
  back intact dsRNA 24 h after a 1 µg dose, with undosed and water controls on the
  same gel. See [Results](/results).
- **10 August** — 120 newly emerged bees prepared from six frames, and 20 unspiked
  larval haemolymph samples banked as matrix.
- **12 August** — a ~700 bp band from adult haemolymph at 3 h, reported with the
  contamination caveat attached.
- **16–18 August** — the 500 bp no-Mango adult batch is lost when bees escape and
  become untraceable; the first formal larval assay starts the same day, at 24, 48,
  72 and 168 h.
- **26–27 August** — haemolymph from 60 newly emerged bees collected for the Mango
  calibration curve.

### September 2026

- **1–3 September** — mite-tray counts across every hive, a sugar shake that fails
  in the rain, and one that works in the dry on hive 14.
- **3–4 September** — the first mite husbandry pilot. Soaked mites on pupae reach
  35.7% survival; mites alone do not survive.
- **5–10 September** — Gibson step 1 gives the expected product, step 2 gives a
  smear, and three rounds of troubleshooting trace it to the wrong band having
  been excised. Four annealed dsRNA stocks are banked on **6 September**.
- **7–8 September** — the four-way housing comparison. With a host 9 of 20 mites
  survive; **alone, 0 of 18**. See [Results](/results).
- **11 September** — Qubit shows the dsRNA soaking dose is far below the intended
  concentration, and a standing rule is set to log NanoDrop and Qubit before and
  after nuclease treatment for every batch.
- **20 September** — the yeast plasmid is linearised and DpnI-treated;
  transformation is **still outstanding at the freeze**.
- **24–25 September** — larvae prepared to host mites for the efficacy screen;
  35 of 40 completed, short on dsRNA.

## The turns that mattered

Five dates did more than the rest.

**29 March — the supervisor rejects the project the team had voted for.** Five
objections, none of them about the science being wrong: the scope was
multi-year, there was little visible synthetic biology, and there was no
directed path to optimise anything. Killing your own project before a single
pipette is lifted is the cheapest design iteration available, and it happened
before the project even existed. See [Engineering](/engineering).

**18 June — there are almost no mites in the frames.** Collecting enough
_Varroa_ for mite assays before August turned out to be impossible, so the
whole experimental plan inverted: dose live bees first, use the results to cut
the target list, and reach the mites later with more statistical power. **A
supply constraint, not a hypothesis**, set the shape of the summer. See
[Bee Lab](/bee-lab#varroa-work).

**23 July — an interview changes the chassis.** A honey company's science and
regulatory leads explained that a strict national ban ruled out the engineered
gut symbiont in that market, and that **price rather than efficacy** decides what
a beekeeper buys, because honey is a low-margin business. Yeast became the lead
platform on regulation and cost. See
[What would a beekeeper use, and what can they afford?](/human-practices#sm-heading)
and
[the chassis cycle](/engineering#cycle-2-2).

**Around 4 August — the first real result.** Whole larvae extracted 24 hours
after a dose gave back the construct intact. Until then, **everything on the
project was design**. See
[Can we detect ingested dsRNA?](/engineering#cycle-3-2).

**3 September — a requirement nobody had asked for.** A recorded debate between
a resistance researcher and a commercial beekeeper produced a design
constraint neither the team nor the literature had raised: current treatments
cannot be used **while honey supers are on the hive**. If NECTAR can be,
beekeepers could treat at the right moment and less often. See
[What would a beekeeper use?](/human-practices#sm-heading).

## What this page is made of

Every entry is reconstructed from material the team produced at the time: the
working channels it ran between 12 February and 23 September 2026, the lab
journals, and the calendars. Nothing here is inferred or reconstructed from
memory.

Interviewees are described by role and organisation rather than by name unless
their profile is already published on
[Human Practices](/human-practices#sm-heading); the conversations still under
review are counted here but not named.
