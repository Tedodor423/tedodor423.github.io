## Key points

- ACRE evaluates the risks of releasing GMOs in the UK, mainly determining **whether the release could cause harm to human health or the environment**
- Evidence needs to be provided that **no harm would be caused**, including that there are **no off-target effects**
- From a UK regulatory standpoint, **the delivery method is secondary to the endpoint**: regulators care more about where the treatment goes and what effects it has rather than how it’s administered
- If components entered honey or other bee products, this could introduce **additional food-safety regulation**

## Why we interviewed

We spoke with Professor Jim Dunwell because, as Chair of ACRE, he could help us understand how the environmental release of an engineered organism would be assessed in the UK. We were particularly interested in comparing our living engineered *S. alvi* system with our non-living yeast-derived approach and identifying the evidence we would eventually need to demonstrate that NECTAR could be deployed safely.

## What we learned

Professor Dunwell helped us understand regulation through **the concept of pathways to harm**. For NECTAR, this means asking where the engineered organism or dsRNA could travel beyond its intended location, which organisms might encounter it, and whether that exposure could cause adverse effects. He particularly highlighted non-target organisms such as other mites and explained that eventual regulatory assessment could require experimental evidence demonstrating their safety. Importantly, he challenged our assumption that regulators would necessarily focus primarily on whether dsRNA was delivered using living *S. alvi* or another system. Instead, he explained that **“the delivery method is secondary to the endpoint”**: where the intervention ultimately goes and what effects it produces can be more important than how it gets there.

## Quote

> the delivery method is secondary to the endpoint

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

His feedback encouraged us to **treat environmental fate and off-target effects as engineering requirements**, rather than assuming that moving to a non-living formulation automatically resolved our safety concerns. We incorporated non-target organisms into our bioinformatic off-target screening and investigated what could happen to dsRNA after administration, including its potential presence and degradation in honey. His perspective also added an important counterpoint to other regulatory conversations that favoured yeast over living *S. alvi*: while our eventual chassis decision still prioritised heat-inactivated yeast, Professor Dunwell reminded us that **changing the delivery system does not remove the responsibility to demonstrate that the therapeutic itself is safe once released**.
