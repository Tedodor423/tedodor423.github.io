## Why we interviewed

We spoke with representatives from Comvita, one of New Zealand's largest beekeeping operations with approximately 20,000 hives and a major producer of Mānuka honey, to understand how a large commercial honey business evaluates Varroa management. Their combined expertise in science, research, quality and regulation allowed us to explore not only whether NECTAR could improve bee health, but also the commercial, regulatory and honey-production considerations that would determine whether an intervention could realistically be adopted.

## What we learned

Comvita highlighted that **treatment decisions are shaped by much more than efficacy**. They identified cost as a major determinant of adoption, particularly within an industry where the value of honey constrains how much additional expenditure producers can absorb. They also encouraged us to **evaluate the effect of an intervention on honey yield**, rather than assuming that improved Varroa control automatically translates into greater productivity. From a regulatory perspective, they highlighted New Zealand's restrictive approach to GM organisms and suggested that our non-living yeast-derived approach could provide a more feasible direction than releasing engineered *S. alvi*. They also discussed limitations of other biological approaches, including poor prospects for using gene drive in Varroa, reinforcing the value of investigating alternative control mechanisms.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

Their feedback strengthened two major directions within NECTAR. First, it contributed to our decision to **prioritise engineered yeast followed by inactivation**, rather than relying on persistent living *S. alvi*, with international deployability becoming an explicit design consideration. Second, their emphasis on cost and productivity fed into our economic and BEEHAVE modelling, where we considered treatment cost alongside colony survival and honey production rather than evaluating NECTAR solely through mite mortality. This helped **shift our definition of success** from simply protecting bees to developing an intervention that could also be economically viable for commercial honey producers without compromising the product on which their businesses depend.
