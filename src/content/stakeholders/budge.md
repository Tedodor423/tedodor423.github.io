## Key points

- Confirmed that **Varroa feeding on bee fat bodies should still expose mites to haemolymph containing dsRNA**, supporting the feasibility of our delivery pathway.
- His advice gave us confidence to **continue with our planned adult-bee dsRNA feeding assays**.
- Supported using the **Mango aptamer fluorescence assay** to quantify dsRNA concentration, which we subsequently tested successfully.
- Recommended testing **different dsRNA lengths (300, 500 and 700 bp)** to investigate whether length affects haemolymph uptake.
- Overall, his advice **validated and directly shaped our bee-lab experimental design**.

## Why we interviewed

We approached Professor Giles Budge because of his expertise in apiculture, honey bee health and Varroa–bee interactions, seeking advice on the design of our honey bee assays. One of our biggest uncertainties concerned **whether our proposed dsRNA delivery pathway was biologically feasible**. Literature suggesting that Varroa mites primarily feed on honey bee fat-body tissue rather than haemolymph raised the possibility that dsRNA ingested by adult bees and circulating in their haemolymph might never reach the mites. If this were the case, we would have needed to reconsider both our bee assays and potentially our wider delivery strategy. We therefore wanted an expert perspective before progressing with these experiments.

## What we learned

Giles helped us interpret this evidence more carefully. Although Varroa do feed directly on fat-body tissue, he explained that **this tissue is bathed in haemolymph, meaning that mites feeding on adult bees could still be exposed to compounds circulating within it**. This was consistent with previous experimental evidence showing effects on mites after adult bees were orally fed dsRNA, giving us greater confidence that our proposed bee → haemolymph → Varroa delivery pathway was plausible. We also discussed how best to investigate dsRNA uptake experimentally. Giles supported our proposed Mango aptamer–biotin fluorescence assay as a logical method for quantifying dsRNA in our samples and agreed that testing multiple dsRNA lengths could help identify whether uptake into haemolymph was length-dependent.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

Rather than abandoning adult-bee oral delivery because of our initial concerns, **Giles's advice gave us sufficient confidence to continue testing the pathway experimentally**. We proceeded with the Mango aptamer assay to quantify dsRNA and incorporated 300 bp, 500 bp and 700 bp constructs into our experimental design to investigate whether dsRNA length affected uptake. His input therefore influenced a key stage of our DBTL process: an uncertainty identified from the literature was taken to an expert, used to refine our understanding of the biology, and then translated into specific experiments designed to test the assumptions underlying NECTAR's delivery system.
