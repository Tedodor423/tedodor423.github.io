- Having engineered *S. alvi* to protect honeybees from Varroa, he provided valuable information on how we might approach **optimising dsRNA yield** in *S. alvi* and **control the T7 system**
- He highlighted methods that could be used to quantify dsRNA, such as a split YFP system, but we decided against this method due to its **low specificity**. This encouraged us to look further into **developing an aptamer-based method of detection**.
- As RNase III would degrade the dsRNA, he discussed strategies for maximising dsRNA accumulation and uptake, such as **knocking out the gene encoding RNase III**, utilising dsRNA that can fold into **paper clip RNA**, or **targeting dsRNA to extracellular vesicles**
- Relating the laboratory work to the real world, commercially kept bees were often regularly supplemented with sugar, thus inducers like **vanillic acid** would be very suitable as they could be **incorporated into the feed**

## Why we interviewed

In the early stages of our project, while we were still deciding between the yeast and *S. alvi* approach, we interviewed Professor Jeff Barrick, who has worked extensively with *S. alvi*, engineering the honeybee gut bacterium to produce dsRNA to tackle Varroa mites.

## What we learned

He provided valuable advice regarding the available toolkit for engineering *S. alvi*, and where we might be able to develop new strategies to optimise dsRNA yield. Firstly, we discussed the available promoter library for *S. alvi* and how different promoters had been tested. We had previously used *S. alvi* transcriptomics data to establish a promoter library that we aimed to test. Professor Barrick indicated that his team had carried out similar work, though unpublished, and had **found no promoter to be stronger than the synthetic CP25 promoter**. However, given that previous promoter libraries were characterised by GFP fluorescence as a proxy for dsRNA, we were still keen to test our promoter library by quantifying dsRNA production directly.

Professor Barrick also provided further guidance on the use of inducible gene expression systems that may optimise dsRNA production in *S. alvi*. One such inducer, characterised in a previous unpublished study, was **vanillic acid**, which has one of the largest expression ranges and is less 'leaky' than other inducible systems. One advantage of an inducible system is it provides beekeepers with the ability to implement dosage control using a naturally used supplement.

We were also keen to explore whether a T7 system could be implemented. Professor Barrick highlighted T7 as a 'beast', and we discussed strategies to adopt a T7 system while minimising its cellular burden. One possible strategy to fine-tune T7 expression within *S. alvi* was the chromosomal integration of a strongly repressed T7 polymerase. As mentioned, our conversation with Professor Barrick highlighted a range of suitable inducers that could be explored in this system.

Professor Barrick also provided insight on how to maximise dsRNA accumulation after expression. Once dsRNA is produced, it may be naturally degraded by RNase III. We discussed strategies such as knocking out the gene encoding RNase III, selecting dsRNA sequences which can also fold into paper clip RNA to resist degradation by RNase III, or targeting dsRNA to extracellular vesicles to reduce degradation. Professor Barrick highlighted that **the effect of knocking out RNase III had not previously been characterised** in *S. alvi*, and hence was an interesting strategy for the team to pursue. Given that no hairpin constructs had been validated in *S. alvi*, we were also keen to explore whether dsRNA architecture (hairpin vs dual promoter design) influenced dsRNA expression.

Another important consideration both when optimising dsRNA expression in *S. alvi* and assaying for uptake and persistence in honeybees is a rapid, purification-light method to detect dsRNA. Jeff shared that he had developed a plasmid encoding a split YFP biosensor for dsRNA, which worked by bimolecular fluorescence complementation, so that dsRNA expression could be detected by the measured fluorescent signal. However, he noted that **it was not very quantitative and was non-sequence-specific**, leaving a lot of background noise in the data. This conversation highlighted the benefit of an aptamer-based dsRNA detection method, as an aptamer-tagged dsRNA molecule provides a more specific measurement with lower background noise.

Lastly, he shared useful advice on how to approach the regulatory aspects of a live *S. alvi* therapeutic. Importantly, Professor Barrick highlighted that *S. alvi* **can also persist in wild bee species**, which would influence our downstream containment strategies. GMOs currently face a complex regulatory structure in the US, and so Professor Barrick also provided some useful suggestions to help navigate GMO regulations.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

Overall, our conversation with Professor Jeff Barrick took us on a journey from R&D ideation to considerations for laboratory testing and real-world deployment. While we did not end up working with *S. alvi*, the information regarding control of the T7 system, measuring dsRNA expression and maximising dsRNA accumulation was still greatly valuable, and **he inspired us to develop our novel dsRNA quantification method using aptamers** to overcome the lack of specificity of previously tested dsRNA quantification methods. The conversation also highlighted the regulatory difficulties associated with a live *S. alvi* therapeutic, **that made heat inactivated and dried yeast a more suitable option**.
