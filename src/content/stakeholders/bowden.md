## Key points

- Helped us understand the **Scottish regulatory pathway** that could apply to NECTAR.
- NECTAR occupies a **regulatory grey area** because our therapeutic is produced using a genetically modified organism, but the final yeast is killed and unable to reproduce.
- **Heat-inactivating the yeast could potentially reduce some of the regulatory challenges** associated with releasing a living GMO.
- The novelty of the technology means its **final classification would need to be determined with the relevant regulatory authorities**.
- Highlighted the importance of **providing regulators with as much evidence as possible**, reinforcing the value of our modelling and safety work.

## Why we interviewed

Following our discussion with the Scottish Government Honey Bee Health Team, we spoke with Laura to better understand how NECTAR might be classified and authorised in Scotland, and how this could relate to the wider UK regulatory landscape. In particular, we wanted to determine whether producing dsRNA in genetically engineered yeast but killing the yeast before administration would meaningfully change the regulatory pathway compared with releasing a living GMO.

## What we learned

Laura explained that **NECTAR sits within a relatively novel regulatory area**. Because the treatment is derived from genetically modified yeast, aspects of GMO legislation could potentially apply; however, our final formulation would contain non-reproducing, heat-killed yeast, creating an important distinction from deliberate release of a living engineered organism. She outlined the different regulatory bodies and pathways that could become relevant, while emphasising that **the exact classification would ultimately depend on the characteristics of the final product**.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

The conversation **strengthened our decision to prioritise heat-inactivated yeast over a persistent living GMO, while also preventing us from assuming that this automatically removes all GMO-related regulatory considerations**. Laura's emphasis on providing regulators with a strong evidence base reinforced our modelling and safety work, so that future applications could provide quantitative information on treatment behaviour, efficacy and potential environmental exposure. Her advice therefore helped us design not simply for scientific performance, but for a product whose safety and behaviour could eventually be clearly demonstrated to regulators.
