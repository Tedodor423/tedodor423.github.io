## Why we interviewed

We spoke with Josette Lewis to understand how Varroa affects the wider agricultural systems that depend on managed honey bees, particularly California's almond industry. Having spent seven years at the Almond Board of California working on honey bee pollination and pollinator health, Josette could provide both an agricultural and industry perspective on the relationship between almond growers and commercial beekeepers. We also wanted to understand what a Varroa treatment would need to look like to be practical at the enormous scale of California's migratory beekeeping system.

## What we learned

Josette described a strong “codependency between the tree and the bee and between the almond grower and the beekeeper.” Almond growers rely on managed honey bees for pollination, while almond pollination provides a major source of income for commercial beekeepers. She estimated that **almost all commercial beekeepers involved in almond pollination must actively manage Varroa** and explained how the parasite has increased the difficulty and cost of maintaining healthy colonies while demand for almond pollination has grown. Crucially, she emphasised that commercial beekeepers may manage thousands of colonies, meaning **an effective treatment must also be inexpensive, simple and scalable**. She therefore saw pollen-patty administration as particularly promising because many beekeepers already use them. Drawing on her experience in agricultural biotechnology, she also expected a living genetically engineered organism to face a more costly and time-consuming route to market than a non-living alternative.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

Josette's feedback helped **shift our focus from designing solely around mite mortality to designing around commercial implementation**. Her support for pollen patties strengthened our decision to incorporate our heat-inactivated yeast into an existing hive-feeding practice, minimising additional labour and making treatment more scalable across large operations. Her biotechnology experience also contributed to our decision to prioritise the non-living yeast formulation over persistent engineered *S. alvi* when considering real-world deployment. Finally, her explanation of the economic relationship between beekeepers and almond growers informed our California case study and economic modelling, allowing us to consider how protecting colonies could benefit not only individual beekeepers but the pollination-dependent agricultural industries they support.
