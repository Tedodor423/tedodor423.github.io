## Key points

- Many of the treatment-free beekeepers have actually used chemical treatments in the past before transitioning to treatment-free beekeeping practices, and **reported lower Varroa counts after this change**
- Given their successes with the treatment-free approach, this led us to **reconsider whether our project was the best solution** to tackle Varroa
- They discussed the nuances, suggesting that their **small apiary size and varied genetics** enabled treatment-free approaches, whereas **treatments were likely necessary for large-scale beekeeping operations** with largely uniform genetics
- This led us to **adjust our project’s target audience** from beekeepers in general **to commercial beekeepers specifically**, and guided our future outreach strategy as we reached out to more commercial beekeepers and honey companies moving forward

## Why we interviewed

Following our conversation with treatment-free beekeeper Mark Sandham, we joined a gathering of around 20 members of the Oxfordshire Natural Beekeeping Group to better understand a perspective fundamentally different from our own. OxNatBees is an informal network of hobbyist beekeepers practising low-intervention and chemical-free beekeeping. Because our project was based on developing a new Varroa intervention, we felt it was particularly important to speak with people who might question the need for treatment itself, rather than only consulting stakeholders already supportive of developing better treatments.

## What we learned

Several members described moving away from chemical Varroa treatments and reported subsequently observing lower mite levels in their colonies. They argued that continually treating colonies could interfere with opportunities for bees to naturally adapt to Varroa, whereas treatment-free management may allow traits such as hygienic behaviour to become established. Most importantly, however, they recognised that their experience might not translate directly to every form of beekeeping. They suggested that **their small apiaries and greater genetic diversity could make natural adaptation more feasible, whereas large commercial operations—with many colonies managed for consistent productivity—face very different pressures**. Their perspective therefore challenged our assumption that a new treatment would necessarily benefit every beekeeper and encouraged us to distinguish between different beekeeping systems.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

Rather than abandoning NECTAR, **we narrowed and clarified who we were designing it for**. Our primary focus shifted from “beekeepers” as a single group towards commercial operations where colony losses, scale, pollination commitments and management practices can make active Varroa control particularly important. This also changed our Human Practices strategy: subsequent outreach increasingly targeted commercial beekeepers, honey companies and pollination-industry stakeholders so that our design requirements reflected the needs of our intended users. OxNatBees therefore provided an important dissenting perspective that changed not the molecular mechanism of NECTAR, but **the problem we believed NECTAR should—and should not—try to solve**.
