## Key points

- In most regions of Australia, **honey is produced all year round**, therefore a significant portion of chemical controls cannot be used due to **honey contamination risks**
- In the northern part of Australia, there are **no brood breaks**, only exacerbating the problem as Varroa mites have continuous opportunities to reproduce
- He mentioned that the key concerns with current chemical controls for Varroa was **damages to honeybee health and contamination of honey products**, therefore our project aimed to overcome these two key challenges by minimising potential negative effects on bee health and evaluating contamination risks
- He also described that **the combination of Varroa and small hive beetle was particularly devastating**, leading us to consider whether we could **expand the scope of NECTAR to tackle pests beyond Varroa**
- He estimates the cost of treatment and labour to be approximately **55-65 dollars per year per hive**.
- He thinks that **varroa knockdown percentage is not an accurate measure** of how effective a treatment is. **Colony survival** and making sure that the treatment does not negatively affect the bees or honey yields is what matters more.
- He suggests that including our therapeutic in a real pollen patty **would be expensive**. Depending on the time of the year, bees don’t always eat pollen very well. Small hive beetles can also get into the pollen patties.
- He is **not worried about off-target organisms or effects**. The varroa mite is very unique, he doesn’t think there will be off target impacts on other arthropods.

## Why we interviewed

We spoke with Dr Théotime Colin because his work focuses on developing non-chemical approaches to mite control, giving us insight into both the limitations of existing Varroa treatments and what a genuinely effective alternative should achieve. His perspective was particularly valuable for understanding Australian conditions, where year-round honey production and, in northern regions, continuous brood production create additional constraints on when and how Varroa can be treated.

## What we learned

Théotime highlighted bee health and honey contamination as key limitations of current chemical controls. He also challenged us to **think beyond mite knockdown as our primary measure of efficacy**: a treatment that kills many mites is of limited value if it simultaneously harms bees or reduces honey production, making colony survival and productivity more meaningful measures of success. He estimated treatment and associated labour at approximately A$55–65 per hive per year. He also **challenged one of our delivery assumptions**, noting that real pollen patties can be expensive, are not consumed equally throughout the year, and may be vulnerable to small hive beetle infestation. Finally, he emphasised that Varroa can interact with other threats such as small hive beetle, meaning colony health cannot always be understood by considering a single pest in isolation.

## How we implemented the advice to change NECTAR

His feedback **broadened the criteria by which we evaluated NECTAR** from simply “does it kill Varroa?” to whether it can protect colony survival and productivity without harming bees or contaminating honey. We incorporated honey bees into our off-target sequence screening and investigated dsRNA degradation in honey to assess potential contamination risks. His criticism of pollen patties also ensured that **we treated this delivery method as a trade-off requiring further evaluation**, rather than assuming stakeholder support made it universally suitable. Finally, his observations about small hive beetle encouraged us to consider NECTAR's longer-term potential as a modular RNAi platform targeting multiple threats to honey bee health, rather than Varroa alone.
