A guide, not a report. Everything here is **advice we can stand behind** because we
did it, got it wrong first, or were taught it by a beekeeper. Each item carries
the date it was learnt so you can check it against the notebook.

Ten words carry most of this page — frame, haemolymph, larva, instar, adult bee,
capping, pupa, nurse bee, forager bee, colony. If any of them is new, they are all
defined in [the glossary of bee terms](/education), in our own words, before you
start here.

## Before you touch a hive

- **Find your apiarists early and keep them in the loop.** Every good decision
  on this project came out of a conversation with the bee lab staff: which
  larval stage to use, that in-house rearing was unachievable, that whole-hive
  dosing was too risky, and the mite assay redesign that saved the last month.
- **Two pieces of paperwork, both slow.** A risk assessment for the dsRNA work
  (drafted and sent for signature, 17–18 July) and an animal-use form. Start
  both before you need them.
- **Do the power calculation before the form, not after.** Ours went in on
  24 August. It is also the honest way to keep the number of bees down: the
  PER assay ended up at n = 5 per group with n = 3 shared controls precisely
  because one control set can serve three treatment arms.

## Planning around a colony, not a bench

- **The brood season ends, and it ends earlier than your timeline.** Our larval
  and adult stability assays were both racing a closing season by mid-September.
  Plan the bee work first and fit the molecular biology around it.
- **In-house larval rearing is not a summer project.** Two to three months of
  training, and "a paper in itself" at the lab that does it. Dose larvae in the
  frame during the natural nurse-bee feeding window instead, days 4–7, and check
  survival at day 10.
- **Do not dose a whole hive.** We ruled it out on colony and queen welfare
  grounds and used localised single-frame dosing with three colony replicates.
- **Staff every session with four or more people.** Setup, cleanup, labelling
  and documentation are separate jobs, and the assay falls over if one of them
  is skipped.
- **Newly-emerged bees have to be planned two days out.** Pull frames, let the
  bees emerge, then use them — six frames gave us 120 newly-emerged workers for
  the following day (10 August).

## Adults: catching, restraining, feeding

- **The proboscis-extension (PER) assay is the way to give a known dose.** Bees
  are strapped into tubes with the head and thorax protruding and fed from the
  tip of a 19-gauge syringe.
- **Do not feed bees loose in a tray.** Our first attempt with 20 foragers lost
  most droplets to the floor or to the bees' own surfaces.
- **Gorilla tape alone does not hold a bee.** Bees slipped out repeatedly
  (14 July) until a strap over the abdomen was added.
- **Feed in 2.5 µl increments, not one 5 µl drop.** A large droplet rolls off or
  soaks in and the dose stops being known.
- **Hold them near hive conditions while they absorb it.** A sealed container
  with a warm, wet towel at the base of the tubes gets you close to 32 °C and
  50–60% humidity, and keeps baseline mortality low enough that mortality means
  something. Our own write-up gives in-hive humidity as 80% in one place and
  50–60% in another, so check the figure you are aiming at against the literature
  rather than against us — see [the bee lab](/bee-lab).
- **Budget the time honestly.** One person strapped and fed 20 bees in an hour;
  two people did 50 in two hours.
- **Stagger your groups.** A full 18-bee timepoint takes about 15 minutes to set
  up, so with a 1 h timepoint each group has to start 20 minutes apart or the
  timepoint is fiction.
- **Newly-emerged against foragers is a real trade-off.** Newly-emerged bees are
  calm and give 5–10 µl; foragers give around 30 µl, but only 22 of 50 survived
  the day. We chose newly-emerged, because they are also the nurse stage the
  biology depends on.

## Adult haemolymph extraction

The method is in [the notebook](/bee-lab-labbook). What **the protocol does not
tell you**:

- **Chill the cage first.** Slow bees are handled quickly and calmly.
- **Cut at the petiole**, the joint between the thorax and abdomen. This is also
  the culling step, and it should be one clean movement.
- **Cut the antennae off** to let the haemolymph out of the head capsule. You do
  not need to pull them out.
- **Where you cut the antenna changes the yield.** Near the tip against near the
  base looked like the main cause of our inconsistent volumes (26–27 August). We
  never resolved it; we pooled samples during calibration so the variance
  averaged out. If you can control it, do, and tell someone.
- **Expect 8–15 µl per bee**, often less. Plan the downstream assay around
  pooling rather than around single-bee volumes.
- **Check the centrifuge is clean before the day starts.** Ours was contaminated
  when we first went to use it (16 July).

## Larvae: finding, staging, extracting

- **5th instar is 7–8 mm and curved.** Learn it from someone in person if you
  can; a photograph is not enough.
- **Look near the already-capped cells.** The queen lays outward in a pattern,
  so the frame carries an age gradient and the oldest uncapped larvae sit next
  to the capped brood.
- **Do not disturb capped brood** when collecting a frame.
- **Use blunt tweezers.** Pointed ones puncture the larva and the haemolymph is
  gone instantly.
- **5th instar is the only easy stage.** 6th-instar larvae are too soft and
  burst. Pupal stages are firmer but have lost the osmotic pressure that makes
  haemolymph ooze out, so they need an adult-style extraction instead. We found
  this out by wasting a batch (31 July).
- **Extraction:** a vertical ventral incision just below the head segment, then
  gentle pressure — pinch between two fingers, or press flat on the board with
  bent tweezers. An entomological pin gives the cleanest, least lipid-diluted
  sample. Expect about 10 µl.
- **Dose without touching the larva.** 5 µl dabbed from a micropipette, tip held
  clear. If a dose misses the cell, record the re-dose; two of our larvae were
  double-dosed and it had to be tracked.
- **Mark the frame so you can find them again.** Coloured lines and position,
  with a non-volatile marker, photographed before the frame goes back.
- **Work fast with an open frame.** Nurse bees remove visibly damaged cells and
  pupae if the frame is out too long.

## Feeders and cages

- **Air bubbles cannot be fixed.** Once a bubble forms while filling, empty the
  tube and start again; trying to coax it out makes it worse.
- **Never force a tube into a crowded cage.** It spills. Discard that cage and
  move the bees to a fresh one.
- **Run an evaporation control cage** alongside the treatments, or you are
  measuring the weather.
- **Wash and return feeding tubes** rather than binning them; we kept a labelled
  bucket outside the lab.
- **Check for escape gaps.** Bees escaped from enclosures during setup, from a
  gappy incubator, and from a batch taped with the wrong tape — that last one
  destroyed a whole timepoint because individuals became untraceable
  (18 August).

## Varroa mites

- **Sugar shake only works dry.** Dust brood frames on both sides with sieved
  powdered sugar, frame by frame, then shake. In the rain the sugar clumps and
  sticks to the bees and the frame, and you get nothing (2 September, Hive 26).
- **Count trays first.** Most of our hives had very low loads; three days of
  tray counts told us which two or three hives were worth shaking.
- **Fine paintbrushes** are the right tool for picking mites up.
- **Mites do not survive off a host.** In our hands, 0 of 18 mites housed alone
  in a capsule survived three days, against 9 of 20 housed with a host. Design
  the assay around a host from the start.
- **Soaking costs you most of the mites.** One round put 140 control mites
  through a soak and 40 came out alive. Soak **three to four times as many** as you
  need.
- **Minimise the pre-soak wash.** It appeared to increase mortality.
- **Mites take over five hours to wake after a soak.** That alone makes a
  large single-day screen impractical, and it is the reason we moved towards
  introducing freshly shaken mites into dosed cells instead.
- **Do not check a capped cell early.** Disturbed mites crawl to the sides and
  the bottom and are lost. Hold to a three-day window.
- **Check mites are alive before you place them**, not after.

## Samples, cold chain and downstream

- **Flash-freeze before transfer, not after.** Two of our samples were punctured
  before they reached the tube and the freeze was imperfect.
- **Wrap dsRNA tubes in tinfoil** whenever they are out of the freezer.
- **Dry ice has its own procedure.** Ours: taped upright, binned after use, and
  a custom ice holder for Eppendorfs so nothing tips.
- **A blood RNA-extraction protocol will not handle larval tissue.** It produced
  a goop; switch to a tissue protocol. One sample threw a large white pellet on
  Vezol addition and needed extended incubation and pre-homogenisation.
- **Low 260/230 ratios are usually the ethanol wash.** Going to a full 1×
  volume, and drying with the lid open, fixed ours.
- **Track which bee was fed when against when it was extracted.** This sounds
  trivial and it is the single most common way to lose a day's work.

## What we would do differently

- Get the welfare paperwork and the mite supply moving in the **first week, not
  the sixth**.
- Order the mite housing consumables before the brood season narrows.
- Log Nanodrop and Qubit readings **at every processing step** for every batch, raw
  and post-nuclease. We discovered a soak dose was far below target only on a
  late Qubit re-check.
- Freeze woken mites rather than discarding them; they are data we threw away.
- Run the no-aptamer control concurrently with the treatment series. Ours had to
  be moved to a separate date and is now harder to interpret.

## The bee biology a synthetic biologist actually needs

This is the subset of honeybee biology our project turned out to depend on, written
for someone arriving from a molecular background.

**The colony and its castes.** A honeybee colony (_Apis mellifera_) is made up of
three castes: a single **queen**, who lays the eggs; thousands of female **workers**,
who carry out all of the colony's labour; and a smaller number of male **drones**.
Workers are the focus of our project, since they are both the adults that feed the
colony and the larvae that _Varroa_ parasitises. A **hive** is the physical structure
housing the colony, and a **frame** is the removable panel inside it on which bees
build wax comb. A single frame can be removed, worked on outside the hive, and
returned without disturbing the rest of the colony.

**The worker life cycle.** A worker passes through four stages: egg, larva, pupa and
adult. On day 0 the queen lays eggs in an empty cell, appearing as small white
rectangles. Eggs hatch after around 3 days, larvae are fed by nurse bees for around
5–6 days, and at that point the cell is **capped** so the larva can pupate
undisturbed, with the adult emerging around day 21. Larval growth is divided into
**instars**, growth stages separated by moults. A **5th instar larva is around
7–8 mm long and curved**, and is easiest to find next to capped cells, since the
queen lays in a pattern that creates an age gradient across the frame. **This was our
primary dosing stage, because it sits just before capping, which is when _Varroa_
enters the cell.**

**Nurse bees and the larval feeding pathway.** Adult workers change roles with age,
beginning as **nurse bees**, who feed larvae, before becoming **foragers** who collect
nectar, pollen and water. Nurse bees consume pollen, stored as bee bread, and convert
it into glandular secretions known as **jelly**, from the hypopharyngeal glands in the
head (Rawn et al., 2026). Jelly is the primary food of larvae, so **any substance a
nurse bee ingests could in principle be passed on to larvae. This is the pathway our
project is attempting to characterise.** Feeding behaviour and jelly composition also
change as a larva develops: worker larvae are fed frequently during the first 48 h,
after which feedings become longer, and the sugar content of the jelly increases
while protein and lipid content falls (Brouwers et al., 1987).

**Basic nutrition and metabolism.** Adult bees obtain carbohydrates from nectar and
honey — we used 1 M sucrose as our feeding solution to mimic this — while pollen
provides proteins, lipids and micronutrients. Pollen also provides **sterols**, which
are essential for brood development and which nurse bees selectively transfer into
jelly (Moore et al., 2025). That paper's rationale for engineering yeast to supply
sterols in a pollen patty is directly relevant to any future team, because **it
indicates that a yeast-based patty is a strong route for delivering a compound to
nurse bees, and then to brood.**

**Haemolymph and the fat body.** Insects have an open circulatory system, so
**haemolymph** bathes the organs directly rather than flowing through vessels. This is
why we sampled haemolymph to ask whether ingested dsRNA had been absorbed. The **fat
body** stores lipids and proteins and, importantly, _Varroa_ feed primarily on it
rather than on haemolymph (Ramsey et al., 2019). **Detecting dsRNA in haemolymph
therefore does not on its own show that it reaches the tissue the mite feeds on** —
see Prof. Giles Budge's answer to exactly this objection on
[human practices](/human-practices).

**Where _Varroa_ fit into the cycle.** Mites enter a cell shortly before it is capped,
then feed and reproduce inside the sealed cell on the developing bee. This is why we
dosed 5th instar larvae, and why larval stage matters experimentally: in our work, 5th
instar larvae could be extracted without breaking, 6th instar larvae were too soft to
extract from, and pupae are firmer and no longer release haemolymph under pressure.

## Finding people who know more than you do

A crucial component of honeybee research, given the field's small size and depth of
expertise, is **having knowledgeable mentors to support an early-stage project.**

**Our experience.** We were lucky to have Professor Geraldine Wright as a resident
researcher and PI in the Department of Biology at Oxford. Early in the project we
reached out to her and expressed our interest in developing a dsRNA biopesticide
against _Varroa_. Initially we wanted to engineer _S. alvi_ to produce dsRNA
endogenously in the adult honeybee gut. Using her industry-specific knowledge she
pointed out the clear issues with that proposal, and gave us specific questions to go
away and answer from the literature, which she also supplied in ample measure.
**Without the direction an expert honeybee researcher could provide, we would likely
have spent many more weeks piecing together experimental ideas and protocols from the
primary literature.**

**Reaching out to researchers.** If your team represents a university, check first
whether there is an entomology department with active honeybee researchers; if so, it
is a simple case of organising a meeting and proposing your idea. If you do not have
direct access to entomology professors at your home institution, **contact honeybee
researchers cited in recent papers.** Almost all have their emails available online,
and many will refer you on to their own PIs and colleagues.

**How to make contact.** For researchers in another country, email. A polite email
framed roughly as below worked for us: who you are and which team; the specific paper
or topic that made you write to *them*; one or two sentences on what the project aims
to do and what you have actually done so far; a specific ask of 20–30 minutes and a
named question; an offer of a one-page summary beforehand; and flexibility on timing.

**Many researchers will be happy to assist an iGEM team, and there is no need for
mentors to be physically present in the same city.** If they can see your data and
spare time for a weekly call, significant progress can be made.

**What we raised, and how.** In the 2026 cycle the Oxford team relied largely on
private donations, raising approximately **£40,000** in direct private sponsorship
from corporations in London, biotech companies and VC contacts who believed in the
project. If your team is unlikely to be fully funded by the university or an external
organisation, **have an entire fundraising team**. Outside grants and official funding
bodies, **warm connections are the most effective route: over 70% of all the money we
raised came directly via contacts of individual team members.** Cold emails worked
only where the recipient was high-net-worth and had themselves done iGEM or
similar. Biotech conferences and investor meetups are occasionally useful for
networking, with the caveat that most attendees are themselves raising money.

**University grants.** A crucial source. The low estimate above is about £34,900, but
**a team using free university lab space and buying at most three Jamboree tickets can
get by on about £25,000**, much of it from an amalgamation of undergraduate-focused
grants. At Oxford those include the Oxford Bursary and Crankstart Scholarship, the
Oxford Travel Supplement, the Student Support Fund Bursary, the University Hardship
Fund, the university funding search tool, and **an individual grant from each of the
39 colleges**. Check what your own university offers and apply to every one, writing
each proposal for the specific funder.

**External grants.** Often **more willing to support a bee project than a general
university fund, since honeybee health is exactly the cause they exist to serve.** We
used the C.B. Dennis British Beekeepers' Research Trust and Bee Disease Insurance,
both UK bodies funding honeybee research. This is not UK-specific: most countries have
national beekeeping associations and federations, bee health charities and trusts,
agricultural and pollinator-focused foundations, and government or regional innovation
funds. **Ask local beekeepers, your bee lab's host, or your national beekeeping
association**, as many smaller funds are not widely advertised. Apply early, since
many bodies meet only a few times a year, and write each application for the funder in
question by explaining how your project addresses the problems their members actually
face. Get in touch before submitting: a short conversation about their priorities
usually tells you whether you are a good fit.

**Private fundraising.** In our experience **far less competitive than grants**,
because it depends on one person or committee deciding your project is worth
supporting. One of our most successful approaches was the Worshipful Company of Wax
Chandlers, targeted because they had substantial funds and a natural interest in bees
given the historic link between beeswax and the trade. **That resulted in a £5,000
donation**, and shows the value of researching organisations whose history or mission
overlaps with your project — livery companies, guilds, trade bodies and charitable
foundations — instead of sending the same request to everyone.

Practical notes that made the difference for us: **ask for a short call before any
money is discussed**, since a conversation where you can explain the problem and
answer questions is far more persuasive than a written proposal; go in with a one-page
summary stating a specific amount and what it would fund; expect corporate sponsorship
to be slower, because it usually needs approval through several layers of management
and depends on marketing or CSR budgets set long in advance; and **if you are in the
UK, make sure donations can qualify for Gift Aid**, which lets a registered charity
claim an extra 25p per £1 from a UK taxpayer and makes a donation more attractive to
the donor. Thank every donor promptly, give them regular progress updates, and offer
recognition such as a logo on your wiki.
