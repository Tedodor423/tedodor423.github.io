Yeast earned its place on three separate arguments that happened to point the
same way: a regulatory and stakeholder one, a manufacturing one, and a specific
biological reason that a yeast cell is a good place to accumulate
double-stranded RNA.

## Why yeast

Five arguments, kept separate because they are separate:

1. **Biology**: the absence of canonical RNAi degradation machinery means
   double-stranded RNA can accumulate intact rather than being processed away.
2. **Industry**: fermentation at scale is a solved problem with existing
   infrastructure, which is not true of most production routes.
3. **Formulation**: a whole inactivated cell may be the delivery vehicle, which
   removes an extraction and purification step rather than optimising it.
4. **Regulation**: a non-living product sits in a materially different
   regulatory position from a live engineered organism released into a hive.
5. **Feed compatibility**: yeast is already something a bee colony can be given.

The regulatory argument did not come from us; it came from the stakeholder work
on [human practices](/human-practices), and it overrode a stated preference from
beekeepers. That is the clearest case of integrated human practices in this
project, and both pages should say so.

## Design requirements

What the chassis has to deliver, stated as requirements rather than hopes: high
biomass, sufficient intact dsRNA per gram of dry cell mass, genetic stability,
tolerable metabolic burden, induction that scales, and survival of processing
and drying.

The second of those is the one that connects to money:
[economic modelling](/economic-modelling) turns an affordable price into a
required titre, which makes it a design specification rather than an aspiration.
