Australia is the recent arrival of our two regions. An industry that had to
build a response to Varroa inside four years has unusually visible constraints,
which is what makes it useful. The question this page answers is the one all
three regions are written against: **how would the requirements for NECTAR
change here?**

## The pest situation

Varroa was detected in Australia in 2022. An eradication programme costing
**A$100 million** failed and the mite was declared endemic. Difficulty moving bees
from east to west has so far held the spread to New South Wales, Victoria and
Queensland (Danny Le Feuvre, Australian Honey Bee Industry Council).

Feral colonies made the arrival worse rather than better. Because forage is
available year-round, Australia's feral population was dense, and under Varroa
and small hive beetle together it **collapsed within weeks** rather than the
projected two or three years — seeding managed colonies with mites and removing
the free pollination agriculture had quietly relied on (Mike Allerton, Amateur
Beekeepers Australia).

The damage shows up in people and in production. Membership of Amateur
Beekeepers Australia fell from 5,000 to 3,000 when Varroa arrived and has since
recovered to about 4,000. New South Wales has lost 35% of its commercial
beekeepers, and **40% of Queensland's plan to leave** within 12 to 24 months, citing
profitability and Varroa control. NSW beekeepers have lost more than 1,200 hives
and seen about a 50% drop in honey production (Wade Ford, Hive & Wellness
Australia). Around 14–15% of Australian agriculture is directly reliant on feral
and managed bee pollination, so beekeeper attrition is an agricultural problem,
not only an industry one.

## Current practice

Bayvarol was the most effective and most popular treatment when Varroa arrived.
It is expensive, slow to install, and now faces resistance; oxalic acid, as
strips or vapour, has replaced it as the treatment most used (Allerton). Two
separate populations resistant to synthetic treatments such as amitraz were
established in 2025, and Elizabeth Frost (NSW Department of Primary Industries)
reports **dual resistance detected**, which puts essentially every legal synthetic
miticide on a short clock.

What is left has boundaries. Organic acids are **not effective in isolation**
(Le Feuvre). Formic acid is hard to use in Australian heat and humidity, and
most of the continent has a long brood period with often no brood break at all
(Frost). Thyme oil leaves a bitter taste in honey (Ford). Off-label homemade
treatments are filling the gap, which Frost does not recommend and Allerton
reports among hobbyists who cannot afford the licensed products.

## Costs and constraints

Varroa management splits roughly 60% product cost and **40% additional labour**.
Formic acid runs to about A$7 per hive, which compounds over operations of
1,000-plus hives, before counting synthetics bought and colonies replaced only
to discover the mites were resistant (Ford). In our own dataset, NSW beekeepers
report about A$22.18 per hive of direct Varroa control plus 0.37 hours of labour
per hive `[CALC]`.

Cost and labour hit small commercial operators of 200 to 300 hives hardest, and
they are being bought out by larger operators with more staff (Allerton). One
processing constraint shapes the product directly: honey sent into Western
Australia is heated to **65 °C for eight hours**, so anything we put in a hive has
to be followed through that (Ford).

## Regulation

Australia's Office of the Gene Technology Regulator gave the clearest
jurisdictional answer we obtained anywhere. A non-living engineered yeast
product **would not be regulated by the OGTR**, because the administered product
contains no living GM organism. Both *S. alvi* versions would be, and an
inducible switch changes elements of the risk assessment without changing the
authorisation route. Effects on non-target species — native bees, mites, other
insects the bacterium or dsRNA might reach — would be among the most important
concerns, and the engineered organism or its products turning up in honey would
trigger additional assessment. The applicant carries the burden of evidence, and
unlike some regulators the OGTR does not weigh a GMO's benefits when assessing
it.

Two further constraints: Australia **regulates chemical products tightly** because
it exports so much of what it makes, and there are currently no regulated RNAi
products in the country at all (Le Feuvre).

## Stakeholder attitudes

Attitudes split, and **not along the line we expected**. Le Feuvre favoured the
living *S. alvi* route for its minimal labour, and suggested it could be
presented to beekeepers by pointing at the bacteria that already live in a hive.
Allerton was drawn to the same route for the same reason, while expecting the
yeast patty to be the more widely accepted product and suggesting we develop
both for two distinct markets. Ford preferred patties outright, because his
beekeepers already feed them and attitudes to a living GMO count against the
bacterium, and asked us to be transparent about the biotechnology whichever way
we went. Frost was blunter: Australian consumers would most likely be against
anything genetically modified, whatever the economic and welfare case.

Frost also raised the objection that has **no technical fix**. Commercial beekeepers
used to synthetics driving infestation to undetectable may find a product that
leaves a detectable alcohol-wash count a hard sell, however good the colony
outcome.

## What an acceptable treatment would need to do

- **Cost first.** Ford's beekeepers rank cost, then effectiveness, then time and
  labour, then ease of use.
- **Work from few applications.** Le Feuvre's ideal treatment is cost-effective,
  perceived as natural, harmless to bee health, residue-free and effective from a
  single feeding.
- **Hold under reinfestation and fit a rotation.** A treated hive is back at
  threshold within weeks when untreated feral and neighbouring colonies reinfest
  it, so mite kill alone is not the metric.
- **Tolerate the climate and the brood cycle.** No temperature ceiling that
  Australian summers exceed, and no dependence on a brood break most of the
  continent does not have.
- **Not require an absence of nectar flow.** Frost's comparison with Norroa,
  which is fed in sugar syrup: the Australian season rarely offers the window it
  needs.
- **Survive the supply chain.** Behave predictably through 65 °C for eight hours
  of processing, and leave honey saleable.

## Implication for NECTAR

The Australian requirements point at the product we built, and at the one part
of it we cannot defend on Australian preferences alone.

- **The patty fits the country twice over.** Beekeepers already feed pollen
  patties, and pollen is the limiting nutrient in much of Australia, so a patty
  can be a colony's main protein source rather than an extra chore (Frost, Ford).
- **The chassis decision is regulatory, not popular.** Heat-killed yeast keeps
  NECTAR outside the OGTR's remit and away from a consumer base Frost expects to
  reject a living GMO — but Le Feuvre and Allerton, the two Australian beekeeper
  voices, **both preferred the living system**. See [the decision that changed the
  project](/human-practices#the-decision-that-changed-the-project).
- **Regional efficacy became a test, not an assumption.** Ford asked how
  efficacy varies with temperature across Australia; that is now part of the
  [bee lab](/bee-lab) and [ecological modelling](/ecological-modelling) plan.
- **Honey is in scope.** Residue testing through processing sits in the [wet
  lab](/wet-lab) because of this region.
- **The model is Australian where it needs to be.** Ford supplied hive profiles
  from his beekeepers, and [economic modelling](/economic-modelling) runs
  Australia separately rather than converting US figures.
