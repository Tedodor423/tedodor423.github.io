## Summary

Australia provided us with a particularly valuable test case for NECTAR. Unlike
countries that have managed *Varroa destructor* for decades, Australia is
undergoing **a rapid transition from historically low-intervention beekeeping to
permanent Varroa management**.

Since the arrival of the Varroa destructor in 2022, Australia has faced
significant challenges in protecting its beekeeping industry and the
agricultural sectors that rely on honey bee pollination. In this case study, we
**examine the scale of these impacts, discuss how our proposed solution could
contribute to Varroa management in Australia, and reflect on how stakeholder
feedback influenced the development of our project**.

## The Australian Varroa Situation

The Varroa mite was **first detected in Australia in June 2022 in the Port of
Newcastle, New South Wales** (1). This was followed by the launch of an
eradication campaign. Examples of measures undertaken in order to eradicate the
mites at that stage include destruction of bees and hives, strict quarantine and
movement controls, and fipronil baiting (2). The operation cost Australia
**AU$101 million** (6) and The Guardian (3) mentions that **approximately 30,000
hives were destroyed**. After 14 months, in September 2023, it was determined
that **eradication of varroa mite in Australia was no longer possible**.

After this, the focus shifted from eradicating the mite to slowing its spread
and improving its detection and long-term management. In February 2024, a
24-month Transition to Management plan was approved by the National Management
Group. The primary focus of the plan was training beekeepers and providing them
with the skills they need to manage Varroa mite and maintain healthy beehives
(4).

Currently, the Varroa mite is established across New South Wales, Queensland,
Victoria, Australian Capital Territory, and South Australia, while **Western
Australia and Northern Territory remains free from the parasite**.

In early 2026 the synthetic miticide resistance was first observed and currently
**all states with varroa mite, except for the Australian Capital Territory, are
experiencing resistance to major synthetic treatments** (1).

## Understanding Honey Industry’s Needs

To better understand the needs, expectations, and concerns of stakeholders, end
users, and experts, we consulted these groups throughout our project.

The first person we reached out to was Danny Le Feuvre, the CEO of the
Australian Honey Bee Industry Council. Our conversation with Danny provided
valuable insight into the industry and helped us realise that **a useful
treatment cannot simply kill mites effectively**. It must reduce repeated labour
and application, remain effective under continued reinfestation pressure, fit
into resistance-management strategies and avoid unacceptable effects on bees,
brood or honey. Existing treatments impose not only repeated product costs but
also monitoring, travel and labour costs, while attitudes towards chemical
treatment can strongly influence adoption. **The most important insight
concerned implementation.** Danny considered a live engineered bacterial system
potentially attractive because persistence inside the hive could support
longer-term treatment. However, he also highlighted the importance of public
acceptance, containment, communication and regulation. A genetically modified
organism released as a treatment would require clear safeguards and a
convincing safety case. **A yeast-derived or non-live product may therefore be
easier to implement**, even if this introduces an efficacy or persistence
trade-off.

To further investigate the impacts of varroa on Australia’s honey production, we
reached out to Wade Ford, Beekeeper Services Manager at Hive & Wellness
Australia. Our conversation with Wade helped us realise **the serious
limitations and problems associated with chemical treatments currently used in
Australia**. Examples include off-label use of formic and oxalic acids, inability
to treat with formic acid in some regions due to high temperature, resistance to
synthetic treatments, and negative effects on the taste of honey observed in
case of thyme oil. The discussion with Wade also showed us which factors are
most important for beekeepers when choosing between different treatment
options. Drawing on his experience working with beekeepers, he says that **cost
is the most significant one**, and making our product affordable should be one
of our main priorities. Wade suggested **pollen patties as the preferred delivery
method**, since a lot of beekeepers he works with regularly feed bees with them.
Additionally, he mentioned that possible negative attitudes towards living GMOs
could cause our other proposed delivery system, which makes use of the
engineered bee gut bacterium, appear less attractive.

## Impact of Varroa on Recreational Beekeepers

Professional and Commercial beekeepers were not the only group affected by the
outbreak of varroa in Australia. To gain an insight into how the pest might have
affected hobbyist beekeepers, we reached out to Mike Allerton, Vice President
and Biosecurity Officer of Amateur Beekeepers Australia.

Mike describes the effects of varroa as devastating, which could be illustrated
by the fact that after it first hit Australia in 2022, **the number of ABA
members decreased from 5000 to 3000**. He believes that among recreational
beekeepers **additional costs associated with varroa are the biggest problem**.
Some people are not able to afford expensive treatments and therefore resort to
off-label, homemade solutions. Mike is also a recreational beekeeper himself.
Before the arrival of Varroa, he used to produce about 500-700 kg of honey per
year. However, like many other Australian hobbyist beekeepers, **he is now
primarily focused on keeping the bees alive**. Mike was particularly excited
about the version of our solution incorporating the engineered bee gut
bacterium, *Snodgrassella alvi*, as it could significantly reduce the labour
intensity. Mike believes that the value of this solution would be particularly
recognised among commercial beekeepers. However, he admits that it would likely
prove less popular among recreational beekeepers. Overall, in his opinion, **if
the yeast patty version of our solution proves to be highly effective, it will
likely become the most widely accepted solution**.

## Regulatory Perspective

In Australia, genetically modified organisms are regulated nationally under the
*Gene Technology Act 2000* and *Gene Technology Regulations 2001* through the
Office of the Gene Technology Regulator. Depending on the product, deployment
could also intersect with biosecurity controls, food-safety considerations if
recombinant material enters honey, and state-level apiary regulations.

OGTR advised us that two of our proposed delivery systems, in which the
engineered honeybee gut bacterium (*S. alvi*) is used, would have to be
regulated by them. The third one, in which the therapeutic is produced in
engineered yeast and delivered in a pollen patty **would not, as it does not
involve any alive genetically modified organisms**.

This reinforced what we had already learned from our Australian stakeholder
work: even where a live bacterial system might provide greater persistence,
**regulatory feasibility could make a non-live formulation considerably easier to
implement**.

## Lessons Learned from the Australian Context

Our initial concept centred on continuous in-hive production and delivery using
engineered *S. alvi*. The Australian case study made clear that **biological
efficacy alone would not determine whether this platform could reach
beekeepers**. Persistence from a living engineered gut bacterium also creates
regulatory, containment and social-licence challenges.

We therefore moved towards **a more deployable RNA platform, with yeast-based
production and a non-live/inactivated final formulation**. At the same time, the
Australian experience established several additional design requirements:
minimise application frequency and beekeeper labour; account for resistance
through multiplexing or complementary modes of action; and **treat safety,
residues, environmental containment and regulatory feasibility as engineering
requirements** rather than questions to address after the biology is complete.

This decision involves **a genuine trade-off rather than an obvious "better"
solution**. A living bacterial chassis may ultimately offer stronger persistence
and therefore reduce application frequency. Conversely, a non-live formulation
may sacrifice some of that persistence while substantially reducing barriers
associated with environmental release, containment and public acceptance.

We therefore expanded the criteria by which we evaluate NECTAR. Our platform
must **balance efficacy, treatment duration, labour, cost, safety, regulatory
feasibility and social licence** rather than optimising any one factor in
isolation. This is particularly important because Australian registration would
require evidence covering efficacy, bee and environmental safety, residues, food
safety and manufacturing consistency.
