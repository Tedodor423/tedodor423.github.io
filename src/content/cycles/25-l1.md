# First DBTL — pilot testing the dosage for larval assays

**Question.** dsRNA needs to be administered to larvae to assess stability of the
dose over the period of capping — the point at which the workers seal the cell over
with wax. How much, in what volume, and can we get the larva back afterwards?

**Design.** In our first iteration of the larval feeding assay, we used a 20 µL
micropipette to feed 5 µL solutions of our 200 ng/µL dsRNA solution. The sole purpose
of this pilot was for us to become accustomed to larval-feeding and larval-extraction
mechanics.

The route itself was settled first. In-house larval rearing was ruled out as
infeasible in the time available — two to three months of training, and "a paper in
itself" at the lab that does it — so we consulted leading entomologists, including
Prof. Geraldine Wright, and agreed that **in-hive dosing was the best approach given
the timeframe**. The regulations around administering nucleic acid to a hive are on
[safety and security](/project-safety).

**Build.** We extracted brood frames from the hive and pipetted our dsRNA solution
on-the-spot into larval brood cells, specifically feeding **5th instar larvae**. We
pipetted 10 samples per test, one a dsRNA solution and one 5 µL of 1 M sucrose, and
promptly returned the frame to its hive. After 24 h we extracted the frame and
practised extracting larval haemolymph.

We chose 5th instar larvae that were in two rows, and marked the first larval
position using latitudinal and longitudinal marks with a coloured pen on the sides of
the frame. Different colours represented notches indicating the starting position of
the 10 test samples and the 10 control samples.

**Test.** Whether the larva takes the dose, and whether haemolymph can be recovered
from it.

**Result.** We found that the **5 µL feeding volume was very low** — it fell as a
small droplet onto the surface of the larva. Due to the understood dynamics of larval
feeding (Heimken et al., 2009) we know that nurse bees directly feed large volumes of
nutrient-rich secretions that completely cover the larva, which the larva consumes in
one go. In our test, our 5 µL droplet neither covered the larva nor could reliably be
placed near to the larva's mouth region.

An additional issue was that extracting haemolymph from the larva by cutting at an
insertion point and extracting fluid with a 19-gauge needle was unreliable. Larvae
would often burst, contaminating the haemolymph with other intestinal fluid.

**What we learnt.** In order to combat these issues, we decided to **feed a larger
volume of solution to the larva and find a more reliable method of haemolymph
extraction**. That is [cycle L2](/engineering#cycle-l2).

**What it changed.** Established in-hive dosing of 5th instar larvae as the delivery
route for every larval experiment on this project, and sent both the dose volume and
the sampling method back for redesign.
