# What efficacy do we actually need, and when should it be applied?

**Question.** Given real hive profiles from our case study regions, what treatment
efficiency would change the outcome, what would that be worth, and does the time of
year the treatment is applied matter?

**Design.** The results from [M1](/engineering#cycle-m1) made clear the need to
calibrate the inputs to those of actual hives in our regions of focus, California and
Australia. The lack of sensitivity to initial VIL and % DWV indicated that the
sensitivity of the hive must lie elsewhere, but conducting sensitivity analyses for
every hive variable would produce very high amounts of unparseable data. Instead, it
was much more sensible to model hives that actually exist in our regions of focus,
giving an understanding of the **microeconomic impact of _Varroa_ on individual
hives**.

BEEHAVE has a built-in _Varroa_ treatment function which sets a treatment duration
and kills a certain percentage of phoretic mites on each day the treatment is active.
That percentage is the **treatment efficiency**. This method is better suited to
modelling chemical pesticides, which have a simpler mode of transfer than our
proposed solution, but we used it as a starting point for this cycle.

**The seasonal question.** Research into existing
treatments revealed that although most pesticides are applied in the fall, this is
not necessarily the best time, as it is when mite populations are at their peak. If
pesticides were instead applied in winter, when mite populations are low, they would
damage mite populations at their lowest and prevent reproduction in the spring. The
reason for fall application is that many pesticides fail in colder winter weather, or
have lower efficacy because bees stay in the hive longer in winter. **Our pollen
patty method, being impervious to the cold and delivered inside the hive, had the
potential to bypass this problem.** If winter or year-round application proved better
than fall application at the same treatment efficiency, less treatment could be used,
making it cheaper for beekeepers. We therefore varied treatment efficiency through
0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.5, across three treatment cycles:

| Treatment cycle | Start day (from 1 January) | Duration (days) |
| --------------- | -------------------------- | --------------- |
| Year-round      | 1                          | 365             |
| Winter          | 1                          | 89              |
| Fall            | 182                        | 122             |

**Build.** We first set the operating parameters of BEEHAVE based on data gathered
from our interviews: key hive characteristics such as the initial number of bees, the
brood capacity and the colony collapse threshold, as well as beekeeper actions such
as honey harvesting and the feeding of sugar syrup and pollen. These were set to
represent key hive profiles within our case study regions, such as the "Australian
commercial hive" profile; data from interviews indicated that hives fitting each
description had similar characteristics, enabling conclusions to be drawn from a
representative profile.

As an initial test, hives were placed in a **non-forage-limited state** — the
surrounding landscape set such that, absent threats like _Varroa_, it maintains a
steady state around the initial number of bees. The purpose was to **isolate the
threat of _Varroa_ specifically**, something uniquely possible in a model. Colony
collapse has many contributing factors and in the real world it is near-impossible to
isolate the cause of any one colony's collapse, which is why an exact worldwide count
of colonies lost to _Varroa_ cannot be obtained. A non-forage-limited model is
perhaps unrealistic, but supplemented with accurate hive profile data it yields
informative results about the direct impact of _Varroa_.

The model was also modified at this stage to include **the death of adult bees due to
_Varroa_ feeding on their haemolymph**, in addition to the death of larvae in brood
cells and the death of adults due to DWV.

**Test.** The model was parametrised for each hive profile with the initial VIL set
to **10%**, reflecting the average seen across the US and Australia. Since M1 showed
the final state is not sensitive to initial VIL, further tests changing it were not
necessary. The model was run for **5 simulated years**, tracking in each year: the
number of bees, the number of mites and resulting VIL, the amount of honey harvested
where applicable, and whether the colony had collapsed. Bee numbers and honey
harvested feed directly into our [economic model](/economic-modelling) for pollination
services and honey sales.

**Result 1: treatment prevents collapse at all levels, and avoids honey losses at
higher efficiencies.** For the North Dakota hive profile, without treatment honey
production drops to **43%** of the non-infested value, as a result of collapse within
2 years. With a treatment efficiency of **0.01**, collapse is prevented out to 5
years. At **0.05**, honey production is restored to over **90%** of the non-infested
value, and bee populations to 80%. Increasing treatment efficiency past this point
shows an asymptotic relationship.

**Result 2: fall treatment is less effective than winter or year-round, and winter
matches year-round.** Treating only in the fall requires a higher treatment
efficiency to prevent colony collapse, so a fall regimen would require greater
application per day for the same effect. A winter regimen prevented colony collapse
to the same extent as a year-round one, differing only in the rebound in honey
production at lower treatment efficiencies.

**Result 3: the efficiency each profile needs.** The most important output was the
treatment efficiency required to prevent colony collapse while holding losses to 10%
in honey production and 20% in bee population:

| Profile                | Year-round | Winter | Fall |
| ---------------------- | ---------- | ------ | ---- |
| North Dakota commercial | 0.05       | 0.05   | 0.05 |
| California commercial   | 0.01       | 0.05   | 0.05 |
| Australia commercial    | 0.05       | 0.05   | 0.05 |
| Australia amateur       | 0.05       | 0.05   | 0.5  |

**The therapy must have a treatment efficiency of around 0.05 to treat the majority
of hives in our focus regions.**

**What we learnt.** All untreated hives collapsed within 3 years and, before then,
saw cumulative honey production drop to **a third** of the uninfested level. This
supplemented what we learned through our beekeeper interviews in
[human practices](/human-practices). The cycle also taught us that **a winter
treatment regimen would suit our therapeutic much better than the fall regimen used
for current commercial pesticides**, which guided further interviews with beekeepers
about whether the time of application would increase labour burden or could be
changed as needed.

**Limitation.** The required treatment efficiency is a useful number, but **it does
not translate easily to our key wet lab outputs** — the obtained titre of mg dsRNA
per gram of yeast, and the mortality rate of _Varroa_ exposed to dsRNA.

**What it changed.** It produced the efficacy target, and it exposed the gap between
that target and anything the wet lab can act on. That gap is what
[M3](/engineering#cycle-m3) exists to close.
