# Second DBTL — the dose volume and the structural arrangement of the larval spiking assay

**Question.** A 5 µL droplet does not reach the larva's mouth and does not cover it
([cycle L1](/engineering#cycle-l1)). What volume does, and how do we know afterwards
which cell held which treatment?

**Design.** In our second iteration of the larval feeding assay, we **diluted the
200 ng/µL solution 4× by adding 15 µL of 1 M sucrose solution**, giving a 20 µL dose
carrying the same 1 µg of dsRNA. Separately, the larvae themselves forced a change of
sampling route: they are soft and prone to bursting, several haemolymph extraction
methods were tried, removing larvae from the frame was difficult, and RT-PCR analysis
of the haemolymph we did obtain detected no dsRNA. Capping adds a further constraint —
we need to identify which larvae had been fed, and remove them after the cells are
capped.

**Build.** We repeated the pilot assay, feeding 10 larvae the 20 µL dose and a
control group of 10 larvae 20 µL of 1 M sucrose solution. We improved the tracking of
larval sample positioning within the brood frame: we **took photos of the frame and
created a graphical overlay** in which individual sampled cones were marked with
differently coloured marks, depending on the sample group.

The whole-larva workflow was developed at the same time: the whole larva is removed
from the frame, flash frozen, and stored for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR. Dosed and
control cells were interleaved across the frame and **randomised by location**, with
frame zones allocated to timepoints in advance and a full cell map recorded before
dosing, so that treatment group and physical position on the frame could not be
confounded.

**Test.** Feed the larvae the required dose, return one day later once the larvae are
capped, remove them from the capped cells, and store for downstream processing.

**Result.** We found that **the larvae were fully covered by the solution, and that
the solution would visibly disappear**. With the advice of our bee lab mentor, we
concluded that the larvae had fed on the entirety of the solution, and that we could
safely assume the dsRNA suspended within the sucrose solution was ingested.

**What we learnt.** Haemolymph extraction from larvae is difficult and unreliable,
and a large number of samples is required — 10 per dose per timepoint. **Whole-larva
processing is faster and more reliable**, and the capping schedule forces it as well
as the extraction difficulty: dosing at 5th instar means every timepoint falls at or
beyond capping, so each sample is harvested as a capped cell, uncapped at collection,
and processed as a whole animal rather than by in-cell haemolymph extraction alone.

from larval haemolymph to whole larvae for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR. Both are what
[cycle L3](/engineering#cycle-l3) and the brood-frame mite assay
([cycle V3](/engineering#cycle-v3)) are built on.
