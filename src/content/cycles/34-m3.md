# Where in the chain is the dose lost?

**Question.** A single treatment-efficiency parameter hides the mechanism. Which step
of delivery limits the outcome, and what dsRNA titre does the wet lab actually have
to reach?

**Design.** The main learning from [M2](/engineering#cycle-m2) was that a critical
treatment efficiency **was not especially helpful to the wet lab**. Unlike chemical
pesticides, the probability of mite death during the treatment period for our therapy
is a function of several variables, each of which could be manipulated to increase
the overall treatment efficiency.

The key variables are **the mg of dsRNA per g of yeast** and **the amount of yeast
applied per day**. The first was the key lab result the wet lab team was optimising,
so determining a critical value for it would inform decisions at the bench. More
importantly, if we were unable to achieve that critical titre, we could instead
increase the quantity of yeast applied to the hive per day — which would raise the
price of treatment for beekeepers. Studying this interplay was therefore important to
both our wet lab and HP teams.

**Build.** We replaced the single treatment-efficiency parameter with a **mechanistic
representation of RNAi delivery**. The updated model broke treatment efficacy into a
series of sequential processes, allowing treatment performance to emerge from the
amount of dsRNA supplied and its subsequent delivery to the mite.

Treatment was defined by three user-controlled parameters: the **concentration of
dsRNA in the yeast**, the **amount of treatment yeast supplied** to the colony, and
the **treatment period**. The amount of dsRNA entering the treatment pathway was
calculated from the concentration per gram of yeast and the amount of yeast supplied.
This dose was then reduced sequentially to account for:

1. the proportion of treatment consumed by bees,
2. systemic delivery of ingested dsRNA,
3. degradation before reaching the mite,
4. transfer from the bee to a feeding phoretic mite,
5. uptake by the mite.

Finally, the estimated dose reaching a mite was converted into a probability of
effective RNAi using a **saturating dose-response relationship**, combined with the
probability of successful target-gene knockdown and the probability that knockdown
results in mite mortality. Treatment therefore no longer removes a fixed proportion
of mites independently of dose.

**Test.** The same test was repeated as in M2 with each hive profile, a VIL of 10%
and the same three treatment regimens. **Six dsRNA concentrations (0.0005, 0.001,
0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1 mg/g yeast) were crossed with five treatment yeast amounts
(35, 175, 350, 1750, 3500 mg/day) in a full-factorial design, producing 30 treatment
combinations.** NetLogo's BehaviourSpace function automated the model repetitions.
Colony collapse, 5-year honey production and bee populations were tracked.

**Result 1: the trade-off between concentration and yeast supply.** The
full-factorial test is best represented as heat maps. For year-round treatment,
colony collapse can be prevented within 5 years at even the lowest dsRNA
concentrations **if sufficient yeast is added**. But if a concentration above
**0.01 mg/g** can be reached, the same collapse can be prevented with **one hundred
times less yeast**. The dsRNA concentration achievable in the lab is therefore the
key parameter. A second heat map shows the relative change in honey production
compared with an uninfested hive, demonstrating how the treatment "rescues" honey
production, against the up-to-33% loss untreated infestation causes.

**Result 2: winter treatment solidified as the best option.** Under a combination of
0.001 mg/g and 350 g/day, year-round treatment restores honey production to 90% of
the original amount whereas winter restores it to 50% — but year-round applies more
yeast overall and is therefore more costly, making that an unfair comparison. Read
broadly, **in almost every case year-round and winter treatment achieve identical
results**, except at those edge cases. Winter treatment is therefore just as
effective as year-round while being obviously cheaper. Both are clearly better than
fall treatment, which requires as much as 1750 g/day of yeast or a high dsRNA
concentration of 0.1 mg/g. **The ability to operate in winter is a unique
characteristic of our treatment**, and this cycle shows it presents an opportunity for
a more effective and cheaper one. Fall treatment would only be used if the change in
treatment time significantly impeded beekeeper adoption.

**Result 3: the breakeven point for beekeepers.** Taking the winter scenario and an
established price for medium-to-high scale fermentation of genetically modified
yeast, **2.33 USD per gram (3.35 AUD)**, the total cost per year follows from the
amount of yeast per day. Our [economic modelling](/economic-modelling) gives the
private benefit of our treatment as **US$47.9 per treated colony per year in the US**
and **A$18.9 per treated hive per year in Australia**. Set against the cost, that
means **if we achieve a dsRNA concentration of at least 0.005 mg/g we can create an
economically viable therapy in the US, and at 0.05 mg/g we could be economically
viable in both the US and Australia** `[CALC]`.

**What we learnt.** These numbers carry a lot of nuance and many assumptions, but
they give a good sense of what needs to be achieved in the wet lab for economic
viability, and a broad sense that our therapy could replace commercial ones at the
same efficacy and at cost parity. The cycle's design also changed how we talk about
efficacy: we stopped treating it as one number the product either hits or misses, and
started treating it as **a chain with a cheapest link**.

**What it changed.** It converts a colony-level efficacy target into **a titre target
for the wet lab** — the chain of required efficacy → allowable cost → manufacturing
cost → required yeast titre — and it is the model the
[yeast work](/wet-lab-experiments#yeast-production) is measured against.
