# Iteration 4 — testing our SEED-designed dsRNA on mites

**Question.** With mites exposed through their host rather than by soaking
([cycle V3](/engineering#cycle-v3)), does dsRNA kill them, and does SEED's
top-ranked target kill more mites than its lowest-ranked one?

**Design.** We fed dsRNA to 5th instar larvae, the stage when _Varroa_ naturally
enters the cell, and then introduced an untreated mite. **This also let us validate
[SEED](/software)**, our dsRNA design pipeline, by comparing its top-ranked target
(**BEST**) against its lowest-ranked target (**WORST**). Six groups: BEST and WORST
each at **50 ng/µL and 5 ng/µL**, plus two negative controls, 50 ng/µL of 700 bp
dsGFP and plain 1 M sucrose.

_Varroa_ preferentially enters the larval cell around the 5th instar, so a mortality
difference resolved there would most closely mirror how the treatment would work in
the field. Mites feed extensively on larval haemolymph from the 5th instar until they
emerge as phoretic mites; by spiking a 5th instar larva, which we have shown retains
dsRNA ([cycle L3](/engineering#cycle-l3)), the mite is exposed by the natural
infestation route. It also removes the reproduction problem of
[cycle V2](/engineering#cycle-v2), where a soaked foundress sometimes produced
offspring that had never met the dose.

**Novelty.** This sort of mite-death assay has not been performed in primary
honeybee literature. Mite soaking (Campbell et al., 2010) and mite-on-pupa rearing
(Muita et al., 2026) are established techniques, but no published study has exposed
mites to dsRNA via a dosed larval host at the natural pre-capping infestation window,
nor validated a computationally ranked target-selection pipeline by direct
best-against-worst comparison. Our design combines both.

**Build.** For each of the four target groups and the two controls, we fed **40 5th
instar larvae**, **240 in total**, 20 µL of the solution: a **1 µg dose** at
50 ng/µL, **100 ng** at 5 ng/µL. Groups were mapped with a digital overlay of the
frame. The larvae were given 24 h without mites to become capped and to digest the
solution into the haemolymph. **A single mite was then introduced to each cell** and
left to feed for **96 hours**.

**Test.** Each of the 240 cells was uncapped and its mites scored as dead or alive.
Cells holding more than one mite, where the mite had reproduced, were still counted
for the dead and live mites present.

**Result: no dsRNA group killed more mites than the controls.** The six groups
differed significantly overall (chi-square, **p = 0.002**), but the difference was
driven almost entirely by the **BEST 50 ng/µL group showing _lower_ mortality than
the dsGFP control (29% against 91%, p = 0.0002)**. Comparing BEST and WORST with
doses pooled also gave a significant difference (p = 0.03), **in the opposite
direction to our hypothesis** `[CALC]`.

**Splitting by larval condition shows what actually happened.** In larvae that were
compromised by the end of the assay, **45 of 47 recovered mites were dead (96%),
regardless of treatment**. More than half of all larvae were compromised in every
group, so mite death in this assay largely reflects whether the host larva failed,
not whether it had been fed dsRNA. Restricting the analysis to healthy larvae did not
change the conclusion: BEST 50 ng/µL (0 of 10 mites dead) still differed from dsGFP
(7 of 9 dead, p = 0.0007), and no other group differed from the control.

**Limitation.** **Roughly 30–50% of cells contained no recoverable mite**, and we
cannot tell whether those mites died, escaped or were never present. Some cells held
two or three mites, so counting per mite treats related individuals as independent.
And only **14–27 mites were scored per group**. Given these limitations and the strong
confounding effect of larval compromise, **we do not interpret the low mortality in
the BEST 50 ng/µL group as evidence that the dsRNA protects mites**; it is most
likely noise. Full data and statistics are on
[results](/results#feeding-the-larval-host-did-not-kill-mites-and-showed-why).

**What we learnt.** This assay **did not demonstrate dsRNA-mediated mite mortality**.
Larval compromise, not target efficacy, drove the outcome, which points at the
larval-feeding protocol itself. Sample size was no longer the problem it had been in
V2, with roughly 30–40 larvae scored per group, but the results were compromised by
the experimental conditions. Harsh weather, the incubation conditions after dosing
and degradation of the dsRNA in the feeding medium may all have contributed, and a
single target may not be enough on its own to shift mite mortality. SEED's ranking
is therefore **untested by this experiment**, not contradicted by it.

**What it changed.** Future iterations should **reduce larval handling and feeding
stress and score mite mortality only in healthy larvae**, before target efficacy or
SEED's ranking can be meaningfully assessed. The handling lessons are written into
the brood-frame protocol on
[experiments and protocols](/bee-lab-experiments): slit the cap rather than removing
it, spread treatments across the frame, and record larval condition for every cell.
That a single target may not be enough is the argument behind the multiplexed
constructs of [cycle 1.2](/engineering#cycle-1-2).
