# Iteration 2 — soaking, then infesting brood cells in the frame

**Question.** Mites survive best on a host in its natural setting, and larvae kept
outside the hive die ([cycle V1](/engineering#cycle-v1)). If soaked mites go into
live brood cells instead, does our vdCHIB target raise mite mortality relative to a
saline control?

**Design.** We soaked mites in dsRNA and inserted them into live larval cells in the
frame. **With limited resources, we hedged our bets on this new method**: no
comparison against other rearing methods, just our target dsRNA against a saline
control, with as many mites as possible in each arm.

**Build.** Mites were soaked for 6 h in 0.9% NaCl containing a **low** or a **high**
concentration of our 700 bp vdCHIB dsRNA, or in saline alone (11 September).
Survivors went one per cell into capped larval cells, so the mite could not escape,
and were incubated for 24 h. We used every mite that survived each soak and **accepted
unequal sample sizes** as the trade-off.

**Test.** Live and dead mites scored after 24 h.

**Result: mortality rose with dose.**

| Group              | Alive | Dead | Total | Mortality |
| ------------------ | ----- | ---- | ----- | --------- |
| Control (saline)   | 23    | 17   | 40    | **42.5%** |
| Low concentration  | 31    | 39   | 70    | **55.7%** |
| High concentration | 18    | 47   | 65    | **72.3%** |

The **Cochran–Armitage trend test**, our primary analysis because the results are an
ordered dose series with a binary outcome, showed a significant trend across all
three groups: **Z = 3.08, p = 0.002** `[CALC]`. In pairwise Fisher's exact tests,
against a threshold tightened to 0.0167 for three comparisons, **only high against
control held up**. So there is statistically significant evidence of a
dose-response, and high concentration is distinguishable from control; high against
low is consistent with the trend but **not independently confirmed**. With three dose
points the trend test cannot tell a linear dose-response from a threshold effect.

**Limitation.** The soak killed most of the mites before the assay began: only about
20% survived it, and in this run 40 of 140 controls were alive afterwards. The
survivors took over five hours to wake, which makes a large single-day screen
impractical. Soaked mites sometimes reproduced in the cell, leaving unexposed
offspring alongside the treated mother, so some scored mites had never met the dose.
And a Qubit check the same night put the dsRNA concentration far below the intended
1000 ng/µL soaking dose.

**What we learnt.** We had our first evidence of a dose-response to vdCHIB dsRNA,
but **the soak was a major weakness** and a key reason for the high background
mortality: mites die in it, and the survivors take too long to wake.

**What it changed.** To bypass soaking entirely, and the fluctuating sample sizes it
caused, we decided to deliver dsRNA **through the host**, by feeding the larvae
themselves: [cycle V3](/engineering#cycle-v3). A next step for this cycle as it
stands would be to repeat it in a more favourable mite season (early August) and run
the 72 h mite titre with larger, equalised sample sizes and a dsRNA concentration
titre to show a linear effect.
