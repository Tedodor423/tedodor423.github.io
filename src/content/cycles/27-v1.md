# Iteration 1 — soaking, then infesting outside the hive

**Question.** Before a mortality screen can run, mites have to survive long enough to
be dosed and scored, against a background mortality low enough to read a dsRNA effect
over. Does a mite need a host to survive outside the hive, and what does the soak
that delivers the dose cost?

**Design.** We started from a method in the literature, Becchimanzi et al. (2020):
rear soaked mites on white-eyed pupae, and compare them with mites kept without a
host in incubator conditions. We wanted to know whether a host is needed at all,
because larvae are hard to extract and hard to keep alive in an incubator. We
hypothesised that a host would give the mites sustenance and so lower baseline
mortality, letting later mite deaths be attributed to the target rather than to
rearing conditions.

**Build.** Mites were collected by sugar shake (3 September), soaked for 6 h in 0.9%
NaCl and given time to recover. Only survivors were used, and roughly **20% of mites
survived the soak**. Three groups: **14 soaked mites on individual white-eyed pupae in
gelatin capsules, 10 soaked mites in empty capsules, and 10 unsoaked mites in
Eppendorf tubes** (the negative control).

**Test.** After 24 h in the incubator, live and dead mites were counted per group.

**Result.** Mortality was **64% on pupae (9/14), 90% in empty capsules (9/10) and
100% in Eppendorf tubes (10/10)**. **Neither comparison was statistically
significant**: one-sided Fisher's exact tests against a Bonferroni-corrected
threshold of 0.025 gave p = 0.17 against empty capsules and p = 0.047 against
Eppendorf tubes `[CALC]`. Pupal rearing showed a trend towards better survival,
enough to steer the next design but not confirmed. The Eppendorf group also differed
from the pupal group in both soaking and container, so its higher mortality cannot be
put down to the missing host alone.

**A second housing comparison, 7–8 September.** Mites soaked for 6 h in 0.9% NaCl,
or left unsoaked, were housed in capped cells in-frame, on white-eyed pupae in
capsules, on 5th instar larvae in capsules, or alone in a capsule, and scored at the
3-day checkpoint: the minimum window, because disturbing a capped cell earlier loses
mites to the sides and bottom. **With a host, 9 of 20 survived (45%)**: 4/10
in-frame, 3/5 on white-eyed pupae, 2/5 on larvae. **Alone, 0 of 18 survived**; Fisher
exact test, host against no host, **p = 0.0013** `[CALC]`. The two alone arms,
soaked 0/9 against unsoaked 0/9 (p = 1.0), cannot separate the soak from the missing
host, because neither kept a single mite alive.

**What we learnt.** Mites are very hard to keep alive outside the hive, and host
larvae kept out of the hive rot and die. A host helped, so rearing on a host looked
the better route. The soak is costly too: post-soak mortality was very high, upwards
of 80% in some batches, so every test and control group needed an excess of mites
collected and soaked to leave enough for rearing. Our standing rule became to soak
three to four times as many mites as the experiment needs.

**Limitation.** Small n in every arm, mites fell off the pupae in the first pilot, and
survival was **scored by movement** rather than by evidence of feeding.

**What it changed.** On the advice of our bee lab mentor, we moved to rearing soaked
mites in live brood cells, a host in its natural setting without capsules:
[cycle V2](/engineering#cycle-v2).
