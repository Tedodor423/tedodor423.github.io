# Third DBTL — feeding the larval host, then introducing the mite

**Question.** Can mites be exposed to dsRNA through their host rather than by
soaking, and does our computationally top-ranked target kill more mites than our
lowest-ranked one?

**Design.** In our third and final iteration, we fed dsRNA to 5th instar larvae — the
stage when _Varroa_ naturally enters the cell — and then introduced an untreated
mite. **This also let us validate our computational pipeline** by comparing its
top-ranked target (**BEST**) against its lowest-ranked target (**WORST**). We
constructed six testing groups: BEST and WORST each at **50 ng/µL and 5 ng/µL**, plus
two negative controls, a 50 ng/µL solution of 700 bp dsGFP and plain 1 M sucrose.

5th instar larvae were chosen because _Varroa_ preferentially enters the larval cone
around that time, so any mite mortality difference we could resolve at this timepoint
would most aptly mirror how the therapeutic would operate in the field. Mites feed
extensively on larval haemolymph from the 5th instar onwards until they emerge as
phoretic mites; by spiking the 5th instar larva, which we have shown retains dsRNA
([cycle L3](/engineering#cycle-l3)), the mite gains exposure in a dynamic that mimics
the natural infestation pathway. It also removes the reproduction problem from
[cycle V2](/engineering#cycle-v2), where a soaked foundress sometimes produced
offspring that had never met the dose.

**Novelty.** This sort of mite-death assay has not been performed in
primary honeybee literature. While mite soaking (Campbell et al., 2010) and
mite-on-pupa rearing (Muita et al., 2026) are established techniques, no published
study has exposed mites to dsRNA via a dosed larval host at the natural pre-capping
infestation window, nor validated a computationally-ranked target-selection pipeline
by direct best-against-worst comparison. Our design combines both.

**Build.** For each of the four target groups and two negative controls, we fed
**40 5th instar larvae** — **240 in total** — 20 µL of the respective solution. At
50 ng/µL that is a **1 µg dose**; at 5 ng/µL, **100 ng**. Groups were mapped with a
digital overlay of the frame. The larvae were given 24 h without mite introduction to
become capped and to digest the solution into the haemolymph. **A single mite was
then introduced to each cell** and left to feed for **96 hours**.

**Test.** Each of the 240 cells was uncapped and its mites scored as dead or alive.
Cells containing more than one mite, where the mite had reproduced, were still
counted for the number of dead and live mites present.

**Result: no dsRNA group killed more mites than the controls.** The six groups
differed significantly overall (chi-square, **p = 0.002**), but the difference was
driven almost entirely by the **BEST 50 ng/µL group showing _lower_ mortality than
the dsGFP control (29% against 91%, p = 0.0002)**. Comparing BEST and WORST with
doses pooled also gave a significant difference (p = 0.03), **in the opposite
direction to our hypothesis** `[CALC]`.

**Splitting by larval condition shows what actually happened.** In larvae that were
compromised by the end of the assay, **45 of 47 recovered mites were dead (96%),
regardless of treatment**. More than half of all larvae were compromised in every
group, so mite death in this assay largely reflects whether the host larva failed,
not whether it had been fed dsRNA. Restricting the analysis to healthy larvae only
did not change the conclusion: BEST 50 ng/µL (0 of 10 mites dead) still differed from
dsGFP (7 of 9 dead, p = 0.0007), and no other group differed from the control.

**Limitation.** Three reasons to read this cautiously. **Roughly 30–50% of cells
contained no recoverable mite**, and we cannot tell whether those mites died, escaped
or were never present. Some cells contained two or three mites, so counting per mite
treats related individuals as independent. And only **14–27 mites were scored per
group**. Given these limitations and the strong confounding effect of larval
compromise, **we do not interpret the low mortality in the BEST 50 ng/µL group as
evidence that the dsRNA protects mites**; it is most likely noise.

**What we learnt.** This assay **did not demonstrate dsRNA-mediated mite mortality**.
The dominant predictor of mite death was larval compromise, which suggests the
larval-feeding protocol itself, not target efficacy, drove the outcome. Our
computational pipeline's ranking is therefore **untested by this experiment** — not
contradicted by it.

**What it changed.** Future iterations should **reduce larval handling and feeding
stress and score mite mortality only in healthy larvae**, before target efficacy or
pipeline ranking can be meaningfully assessed. The handling lessons are written into
the brood-frame protocol on
[experiments and protocols](/bee-lab-experiments): slit the cap rather than removing
it, spread treatments across the frame, and record larval condition for every cell.
