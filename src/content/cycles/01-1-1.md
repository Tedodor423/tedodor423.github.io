# Iteration 1 — in silico design of the dsRNA

**Question.** Which gene, which region of that gene, and what length? Nothing at the
bench separates a well-chosen target from a badly chosen one until months have been
spent on it, so we wanted those choices made _in silico_ first.

**Design.** RNAi proceeds by Dicer cleaving dsRNA into siRNAs, which load onto AGO
and guide it to degrade the target transcript, so efficacy is largely decided by
sequence features that can be computed. We built **SEED (Selective enginEEring of
dsRNA)**, a framework that picks both the target gene and the region within it, in
three stages:

1. **Target gene selection** — functional connectivity, abundance, and direct RNAi
   effects reported in the literature.
2. **Within-gene region selection** — siRNA abundance, Dicer processing, and
   accessibility of the site in the target transcript.
3. **Off-target analysis** — the selected regions against the _Apis mellifera_
   transcriptome.

Between them the three stages cover the bottlenecks that beekeepers, environmental
agencies and regulators kept returning to in conversation: rational target selection,
off-target screening, interaction with native RNAi machinery, single-target
resistance, and environmental degradation.

Length is the other variable fixed at design time, and the literature ties it to
stability, so alongside the targeting constructs we designed non-targeting GFP dsRNA
at **300, 500 and 700 bp** as the substrate for uptake and stability work in adults
and larvae.

**Build.** The pipeline is the dry lab's — [cycle D1](/engineering#cycle-d1), with the
run and its outputs on [software](/software). STRING protein-interaction data gave
functional network connectivity, transcriptomic data ranked genes by abundance, and
published _Varroa_ RNAi studies supplied the genes with experimentally confirmed
knockdown, which we kept as a reference point against SEED's own candidates. For
within-gene design, the raw files of a published _Varroa_ siRNA dataset were used to
establish the features of the mite's own siRNA population — efficient siRNA
production depends on species-specific Dicer processing, which is sensitive to
terminal nucleotide composition, strand symmetry and siRNA length. On that basis SEED
applies a regression model to estimate accumulation from sequence features,
calculates thermodynamic asymmetry and self-folding with ViennaRNA, and estimates
accessibility within the target mRNA with RNAplfold. Off-target analysis maps
candidate regions to the _Apis mellifera_ transcriptome by sequence alignment. The
analysis scripts were wrapped in a graphical interface, so gene selection, region
design and the off-target screen run as a single workflow.

At the bench, the selected regions and the GFP length series were made as
conventional, single-gene, unlooped duplexes by in vitro transcription
([cycle 2.1](/engineering#cycle-2-1)).

**Test.** SEED-informed dsRNA went into three assays, all on real hives at the
University of Oxford's John Krebs Field Station: a mite screen for killing efficiency,
adult feeding for uptake and length-dependent stability, and larval feeding for
stability over the period of capping. The assays themselves are on
[bee lab](/bee-lab); the quantification behind them is
[cycle 3.3](/engineering#cycle-3-3).

**Result.** The mite screen was **inconclusive**: baseline mite mortality and larval
death were both high. In larvae, **the majority of the 1 µg dose disappears within 24
hours** of feeding.

**What we learnt.** Several things could account for the mite screen, and we cannot
separate them: harsh weather, the incubation conditions after dosing, and degradation
of the dsRNA in the feeding medium. A single target may also not be enough on its own
to shift mite mortality, which is the argument that produced the multiplexed
constructs of [cycle 1.2](/engineering#cycle-1-2).

The larval figure points the same way. Poor dsRNA stability in the feeding medium and
in the hive accounts for the low recovery, and dose accuracy is part of that problem
rather than a separate one: Qubit and NanoDrop reported different concentrations for
the same preparation, which made accurate feeding solutions hard to prepare —
[cycle 2.6](/engineering#cycle-2-6).

**What it changed.** Stability, not potency, became the thing to engineer, and
architecture is the lever we control at the DNA level for no marginal cost. We went
looking for a molecule with no free ends for exonucleases to start on, and for a way
to silence more than one gene at a time. That is
