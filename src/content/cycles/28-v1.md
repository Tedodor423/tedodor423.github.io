# First DBTL — a method to rear mites for the mite death assay

**Question.** Before a mortality screen can run, mites have to survive in the lab
long enough to be dosed and scored. How do you keep a _Varroa_ mite alive, and does
the soak that delivers the dose kill it?

**Design.** In our first iteration, we tested whether rearing soaked mites on a host
would lower baseline mortality, so that mite deaths in later dsRNA assays could be
attributed to the target rather than to rearing conditions. Following Becchimanzi et
al. (2020), we compared mites reared on white-eyed pupae against mites kept without a
host in incubator conditions. We hypothesised that rearing mites on pupae would
provide them sustenance and hence increase survivability, so that later mite deaths
could be attributed to the target itself rather than to baseline mortality.

**Build.** Mites were soaked for 6 h in 0.9% NaCl and given time to recover. Only
survivors were used, as roughly **20% of mites survived soaking**. We set up three
groups: **14 soaked mites on individual white-eyed pupae in gelatin capsules, 10
soaked mites in empty capsules, and 10 unsoaked mites in Eppendorf tubes** (the
negative control).

**Test.** After 24 h in incubated conditions, the number of live and dead mites per
group was recorded.

**Result.** Mortality was **64% on pupae (9/14), 90% in empty capsules (9/10) and
100% in Eppendorf tubes (10/10)**. Soaking mites and placing them on pupae led to the
least baseline mortality of the three conditions, which fits the hypothesis that
rearing mites on a host gives them sustenance and improves survival.

**Statistics.** We used Fisher's exact test, which suits small sample sizes with a
binary outcome. Because we hypothesised in advance that pupal rearing would _lower_
mortality, we used one-sided tests, and corrected for running two comparisons by
lowering the threshold from 0.05 to 0.025 (Bonferroni). **Neither comparison reached
significance**: group 1 against group 2 gave **p = 0.17**, and group 1 against group
3 gave **p = 0.047**, which falls below 0.05 but not below the corrected 0.025
`[CALC]`.

**We therefore treat this result as directional rather than conclusive.** Pupal
rearing showed a clear trend towards improved survival, enough to guide the next
design cycle, but larger samples would be needed to confirm it. The comparison with
group 3 is also confounded: those mites differed from group 1 in both soaking and
container type, so their higher mortality cannot be attributed to the absence of a
host alone.

**A second housing comparison, 7–8 September.** Mites collected by sugar shake were
soaked for 6 h in 0.9% NaCl or left unsoaked, then housed in capped cells in-frame,
on white-eyed pupae in capsules, on 5th-instar larvae in capsules, or alone in a
capsule, and scored at the 3-day checkpoint — the minimum window, because disturbing
a capped cell earlier loses mites to the sides and bottom. **With a host, 9 of 20
survived (45%)** — 4/10 in-frame, 3/5 on white-eyed pupae, 2/5 on larvae. **Alone, 0
of 18 survived (0%)**; Fisher exact test, host against no host, **p = 0.0013**. The
two alone arms isolate the soak and exonerate it: soaked 0/9 against unsoaked 0/9,
**p = 1.0** `[CALC]`.

**What we learnt.** A host is required, and the soak is not what kills them — which
is the result that matters, because the soak was the delivery route. Two further
husbandry rules came out of the same fortnight: soak three to four times as many
mites as you need, because losses in the soak are heavy and variable (one round lost
100 of 140 controls), and mites take five or more hours to wake after a soak, which
makes a same-day large screen impractical.

**Limitation.** Small n in every arm, mites fell off pupae in the earlier pilot, and
survival was **scored by movement** rather than by evidence of feeding.

**What it changed.** On the advice of our bee lab mentor, we moved to rearing soaked
mites in live brood cells, providing a host in its natural setting without capsules.
That is [cycle V2](/engineering#cycle-v2).
