# Does it kill the mite?

**Question.** Before a mortality screen can run, mites have to survive in the lab
long enough to be dosed and scored. How do you keep a _Varroa_ mite alive?

**Design.** The dosing route we need is a soak, so the two things to separate are
whether the soak kills mites and whether the housing does. Four housing methods
were compared with the soak crossed against them.

**Build.** Mites collected by sugar shake, soaked 6 h in 0.9% NaCl or left
unsoaked, then housed in capped cells in-frame, on white-eyed pupae in cellulose
capsules, on 5th-instar larvae in capsules, or alone in a capsule. Scored at the
3-day checkpoint, which is the minimum window: disturbing a capped cell earlier
loses mites to the sides and bottom.

**Test and result (7–8 Sep).** **With a host, 9 of 20 survived (45%)** — 4/10
in-frame, 3/5 on white-eyed pupae, 2/5 on larvae. **Alone, 0 of 18 survived (0%)**.
Fisher exact test, host against no host: **p = 0.0013** `[CALC]`. The two alone arms
isolate the soak, and exonerate it: soaked 0/9 against unsoaked 0/9, **p = 1.0**
`[CALC]`. An earlier pilot (3–4 Sep) gave 35.7% survival for soaked mites on pupae
across 10 replicates, with the no-pupa and unsoaked-alone arms near zero.

**What we learnt.** A host is required and the soak is not what kills them, which
is the result that matters, because the soak is the delivery route. Two further
husbandry rules came out of the same fortnight: soak three to four times as many
mites as you need, because losses in the soak are heavy and variable (one round
lost 100 of 140 controls), and mites take five or more hours to wake after a soak,
which makes a same-day large screen impractical.

**Limitation.** Small n in every arm, mites fell off pupae in the earlier pilot,
and survival was **scored by movement** rather than by evidence of feeding.
