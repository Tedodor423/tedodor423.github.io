# First DBTL — the most effective assay to feed adult bees a dsRNA-containing sucrose solution

**Question.** How much dsRNA does a bee actually receive? Every µg-per-bee figure
on this wiki depends on the answer, and a cage of bees sharing a feeder does not
give one. **A dose that is not known cannot be used as the denominator of an uptake
measurement.**

**Design.** Testing a range of salt concentrations to resolve any effect on bee
mortality, and piloting the cage feeding assay method. The pilot also estimated how
much 1 M sucrose a cage of bees consumes in 24 h, which indicates to us the volume of
dsRNA solution a full assay would need. The salt arms were included because dsRNA
stability is favoured at 100–150 mM, so whether that salt load deters feeding had to
be established before any formulation decision could be made.

**Build.** Each mini-cage held bees and four 2 ml Eppendorf feeding tubes. The tubes
held 1 M sucrose, alone or with 50, 100 or 150 mM NaCl. Tubes were weighed empty,
filled to the brim, weighed again, and inserted into the cage. Fifteen cages in all:
boxes 1–5 on plain sucrose, boxes 7–9 at 50 mM, 10–12 at 100 mM, 13–15 at 150 mM, and
**box 6 run bee-free as the evaporation control**. Tubes were weighed, replaced and
re-weighed daily over three days, 30 June – 3 July, and dead bees were counted at
every change and folded into the per-bee denominator.

**Test.** Cages were kept in the bee-lab incubator for 24 h at a time. The tubes were
then removed and re-weighed on the same balance to calculate consumption, against the
bee-free control.

**Result: three-quarters of what the balance weighed was evaporation** `[CALC]`.

| Interval | Gross loss per cage | Evaporation control | Evaporation as % of gross | Net consumption |
| -------- | ------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------- |
| Day 1–2  | 0.976 g             | 0.879 g             | **90%**                   | 0.097 g         |
| Day 2–3  | 1.282 g             | 0.972 g             | **76%**                   | 0.310 g         |
| Day 3–4  | 1.219 g             | 0.712 g             | **58%**                   | 0.507 g         |
| **Mean** | 1.092 g             | 0.854 g             | **74%**                   | 0.305 g         |

On the first day the figure was 90%, meaning the quantity of interest was one tenth
of the quantity being weighed.

**Result: salt is not a constraint.** Once corrected for evaporation, intake was
stable across the salt series `[CALC]`:

| Treatment     | g/bee/day | µL/bee/day |
| ------------- | --------- | ---------- |
| 1 M sucrose   | 0.0205    | **16.7**   |
| + 50 mM NaCl  | 0.0193    | 15.7       |
| + 100 mM NaCl | 0.0192    | 15.6       |
| + 150 mM NaCl | 0.0203    | 16.5       |

The 150 mM figure is reported **with box 13 excluded**. That cage was recorded at the
bench as having suffered spillage, returned a day-1 gross loss of 2.665 g against a
treatment mean of 1.22 g, and lost 5 of 15 bees. Included, the arm reads 27.9
µL/bee/day and appears to show salt-driven hyperphagia; excluded, it reads 16.5 and
the apparent effect disappears.

Sucrose consumption did not differ across 0, 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl — one-way ANOVA
on cage means, **F(3,10) = 0.96, p = 0.45** — and 24 h mortality was not associated
with it: 3 deaths in 210 bees, **chi-square = 4.85 on 3 df, p = 0.18** `[CALC]`.
**Across 0–150 mM NaCl no deterrence was detected.** The salt concentration that
dsRNA stability requires is accepted by the animal, and that is a reusable
formulation result.

**Result: the dead volume, which ended the design.** Of 8 mL loaded per cage per day,
a mean of 0.248 mL was drunk `[CALC]`:

|               | Drunk        | Loaded    | Utilisation |
| ------------- | ------------ | --------- | ----------- |
| Day 1–2       | 0.079 mL     | 8 mL      | 0.98%       |
| Day 2–3       | 0.252 mL     | 8 mL      | 3.15%       |
| Day 3–4       | 0.412 mL     | 8 mL      | 5.15%       |
| **3-day run** | **0.742 mL** | **24 mL** | **3.1%**    |

Because tubes were replaced daily, the dead volume was paid again every day:
**96.9% of everything loaded was discarded or unconsumed.**

Translated into dsRNA production this is not an inefficiency but a wall. At the
measured 16.7 µL/bee/day, delivering 1 µg per bee per day requires **60 ng/µL**:

| Quantity               | Value        | How it arises                                |
| ---------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Concentration required | 60 ng/µL     | 1000 ng ÷ 16.7 µL consumed per bee per day   |
| Volume loaded          | 24 mL        | 8 mL per cage × 3 daily replacements          |
| **dsRNA loaded**       | **1.44 mg**  | 24 mL × 60 ng/µL                             |
| **dsRNA delivered**    | **45 µg**    | 15 bees × 1 µg × 3 days                       |
| Waste ratio            | **32:1**     | 1.44 mg loaded per 45 µg reaching an animal  |

A pair of 20 µL high-yield IVT reactions, one per strand, yields an annealed duplex
stock of roughly 90 µg (the four stocks produced on 6 September were 88–93 µg each).
Supplying one cage for three days therefore requires about **16 annealing runs, or 32
IVT reactions**. Scaled to the smallest design worth running — three arms, three
replicate cages each — the requirement is **12.9 mg, about 288 IVT reactions**, or
roughly three complete 100-reaction kits `[LIT]` consumed to generate a single
dataset. Synthesis, not bees, becomes the binding constraint.

**The degradation multiplier.** dsRNA in sucrose has been reported with
t½ ≈ 0.66 h (Muita 2026, measured in 50% sucrose; ours was 1 M, ~34% w/v, so the
transfer is an extrapolation) `[LIT]`. Integrating first-order decay across a 24 h
feeding window gives a mean intact fraction of **4.0%** of what is drunk (15.8%
across 6 h). Delivering 1 µg of _intact_ duplex would then need a 1.5 µg/µL feeding
solution, or **36 mg per cage per run** `[CALC]`.

**What we learnt.** Cage feeding gives a population mean and cannot support a
per-bee figure, however good the feeder. Intra-cage intake has been reported to vary
roughly 8.8-fold between bees sharing a cage `[LIT]`, and 25–45% of ingested sugar is
redistributed by trophallaxis within 48 h. Both were known before the pilot; neither
is why the design was dropped. **The reason was that the dose could not be afforded,
and 74% of the readout was water leaving a tube.**

Three practical lessons came out of the same fortnight. Air bubbles often formed
while filling tubes, and trying to remove a bubble usually made it worse, so emptying
and refilling from scratch was more reliable. Forcing a new tube into a crowded cage
during changeovers caused major spillage, so affected bees were moved to a fresh cage
instead. And 1–2 bees would occasionally die within 24 h, rising to 4 over 48 h,
which reduces the volume fed that day; this was accounted for by adding the previous
day's average intake per bee multiplied by the number that had died.

**Limitation.** The weekend background figures were 0/15, 1/16 and 2/15 across three
cage boxes (20 Jul). Three of the four expected cell counts in the mortality
chi-square fall below 5, so that test is approximate.

**What it changed.** The salt result cleared our dsRNA stock buffer for use in every
later feeding experiment, and **the volume of dsRNA needed to fill 2 ml tubes at our
target concentration was too high to produce by IVT for every cage**, so the feeding
tubes had to be scaled down — [cycle B2](/engineering#cycle-b2). Larval dosing moved
to in-frame spiking for the same reason: the larva stays in the colony and the dose
is applied to its cell.
