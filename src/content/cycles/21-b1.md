# Iteration 1 — cage feeding: does the salt in our dsRNA buffer change uptake?

**Question.** Our dsRNA buffer carries NaCl. Does that salt change how much sucrose
solution a bee drinks, or how many bees die? And can a cage of bees sharing a feeder
tell us how much dsRNA each bee received? Every µg-per-bee figure on this wiki
depends on the second answer: **a dose that is not known cannot be the denominator of
an uptake measurement.**

**Design.** We fed caged bees a range of salt concentrations to resolve any effect on
intake and mortality, and used the same run as a pilot of the cage-feeding assay. The
salt arms were there because dsRNA stability is favoured at 100–150 mM, so whether
that salt load deters feeding had to be settled before any formulation decision. The
pilot also estimated how much 1 M sucrose a cage drinks in 24 h, which sets the
volume of dsRNA solution a full assay would need.

**Build, in two rounds.**

1. **2 mL feeders, 30 June to 3 July.** Each mini-cage held bees and four 2 mL
   Eppendorf feeding tubes of 1 M sucrose, alone or with 50, 100 or 150 mM NaCl.
   Tubes were weighed empty, filled to the brim, weighed again and inserted. Fifteen
   cages: boxes 1–5 on plain sucrose, 7–9 at 50 mM, 10–12 at 100 mM, 13–15 at
   150 mM, and **box 6 run bee-free as the evaporation control**. Tubes were weighed,
   replaced and re-weighed daily for three days, and dead bees were counted at every
   change and folded into the per-bee denominator.
2. **PCR-tube feeders in 3D-printed inserts, July.** The first round showed that 2 mL
   tubes need more dsRNA than our IVT could make for every cage (below), so a 0.2 mL
   PCR tube was seated in a 3D-printed insert in their place, in **three geometries:
   horizontal, tilted at 15° and vertical**. Each tube had two feeding ports made with
   sterile hypodermic needles, **one near the base and one at the neck**; the upper
   port breaks the airlock that otherwise stops flow as the reservoir drains. Port
   diameter was set at **20, 21 and 23 gauge** (0.90, 0.82 and 0.64 mm outer
   diameter). The horizontal tube was rejected on handling before any measurement:
   any touch to its underside discharged it, and it spilled when cage trays were
   carried. The other two ran as **2 geometries × 3 gauges × 3 replicates**, 18 cages
   of 15 bees (270 animals) plus **five bee-free evaporation controls**, weighed over
   two days, with the perforated 2 mL Eppendorf kept as the comparison.

**Test.** Cages spent 24 h at a time in the bee-lab incubator. The tubes were then
re-weighed on the same balance and consumption calculated against the bee-free
controls.

**Result: salt is not a constraint.** There was **no significant dose-response**
between sucrose intake and NaCl concentration, and no effect of NaCl on mortality.
Corrected for evaporation `[CALC]`:

| Treatment     | g/bee/day | µL/bee/day |
| ------------- | --------- | ---------- |
| 1 M sucrose   | 0.0205    | **16.7**   |
| + 50 mM NaCl  | 0.0193    | 15.7       |
| + 100 mM NaCl | 0.0192    | 15.6       |
| + 150 mM NaCl | 0.0203    | 16.5       |

The 150 mM figure is reported **with box 13 excluded**. That cage was recorded at the
bench as having suffered spillage, returned a day-1 gross loss of 2.665 g against a
treatment mean of 1.22 g, and lost 5 of 15 bees. Included, the arm reads 27.9
µL/bee/day and appears to show salt-driven hyperphagia; excluded, it reads 16.5 and
the apparent effect disappears.

One-way ANOVA on cage means, **F(3,10) = 0.96, p = 0.45**; 24 h mortality, 3 deaths
in 210 bees, **chi-square = 4.85 on 3 df, p = 0.18** `[CALC]`. Three of the four
expected cell counts in the chi-square fall below 5, so that test is approximate.
Background mortality in cages was low: 0/15, 1/16 and 2/15 across three boxes over a
weekend (20 July). **The salt concentration dsRNA stability requires is accepted by
the bee**, and that is a reusable formulation result.

On the first day the quantity of interest was one tenth of the quantity being
weighed.

**Result: the dead volume, which ended the 2 mL design.** Of 8 mL loaded per cage per
day, a mean of 0.248 mL was drunk `[CALC]`:

|               | Drunk        | Loaded    | Utilisation |
| ------------- | ------------ | --------- | ----------- |
| Day 1–2       | 0.079 mL     | 8 mL      | 0.98%       |
| Day 2–3       | 0.252 mL     | 8 mL      | 3.15%       |
| Day 3–4       | 0.412 mL     | 8 mL      | 5.15%       |
| **3-day run** | **0.742 mL** | **24 mL** | **3.1%**    |

Tubes were replaced daily, so the dead volume was paid again every day: **96.9% of
everything loaded was discarded or unconsumed.** At the measured 16.7 µL/bee/day,
delivering 1 µg per bee per day requires **60 ng/µL**:

| Quantity               | Value       | How it arises                               |
| ---------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Concentration required | 60 ng/µL    | 1000 ng ÷ 16.7 µL consumed per bee per day  |
| Volume loaded          | 24 mL       | 8 mL per cage × 3 daily replacements        |
| **dsRNA loaded**       | **1.44 mg** | 24 mL × 60 ng/µL                            |
| **dsRNA delivered**    | **45 µg**   | 15 bees × 1 µg × 3 days                     |
| Waste ratio            | **32:1**    | 1.44 mg loaded per 45 µg reaching an animal |

A pair of 20 µL high-yield IVT reactions, one per strand, yields an annealed duplex
stock of roughly 90 µg (the four stocks produced on 6 September were 88–93 µg each).
One cage for three days therefore needs about **16 annealing runs, or 32 IVT
reactions**. Scaled to the smallest design worth running, three arms of three
replicate cages, the requirement is **12.9 mg, about 288 IVT reactions**, or roughly
three complete 100-reaction kits `[LIT]` for a single dataset. Synthesis, not bees,
becomes the binding constraint.

dsRNA in sucrose has also been reported with t½ ≈ 0.66 h (Muita 2026, measured in 50%
sucrose; ours was 1 M, ~34% w/v, so the transfer is an extrapolation) `[LIT]`.
Integrating first-order decay across a 24 h feeding window gives a mean intact
fraction of **4.0%** of what is drunk (15.8% across 6 h). Delivering 1 µg of _intact_
duplex would then need a 1.5 µg/µL feeding solution, or **36 mg per cage per run**
`[CALC]`.

| 15° tilted       | 21G   | 0.395            | 0.318   | 0.0212    | **17.2**   |
| 15° tilted       | 23G   | 0.430            | 0.353   | 0.0236    | **19.2**   |
| Vertical         | 20G   | 0.540            | 0.438   | 0.0292    | 23.7 ⚠     |
| Vertical         | 21G   | 0.549            | 0.447   | 0.0298    | 24.2 ⚠     |
| Vertical         | 23G   | 0.327            | 0.224   | 0.0149    | 12.2 ⚠     |

⚠ Box 17 was annotated as unusable at the bench and is excluded throughout. Box 21 is
excluded from the vertical evaporation sign as a catastrophic leak.

**Evaporation fell eleven-fold**, to 0.077 g/cage/day for the tilted geometry against
0.854 for the 2 mL tube, and **volume at risk fell twenty-fold**, 0.4 mL loaded per
cage against 8 mL. The tilted geometry returned 15.7–19.2 µL/bee/day against the
16.7 µL/bee/day benchmark, so **consumption was unchanged**. Had it fallen, the
improvement would have been indistinguishable from bees unable to feed.

Both PCR-tube geometries lost far less than the 2 mL tube, but **the vertical one
leaked**: leak annotations were recorded on nine vertical cages and on no tilted
cage, and its apparent consumption at 20G and 21G runs ~45% above the tilted arm,
which is the leak being weighed as intake. Both were still prone to air bubbles and
spillage. Within the tilted geometry, consumption rose as the bore narrowed, 15.7 →
17.2 → 19.2 µL/bee/day across 20G → 21G → 23G: a wider port loses solution by wicking
and dripping before it can be drunk, and tube-emptying was annotated on one 20G cage
and six 21G/23G cages. **Tilted at 23G was adopted.** At 19.2 µL/bee/day fifteen
bees need **288 µL/day** against the **400 µL** in two PCR tubes, 72% of capacity a
day, so uneven draw empties one tube before the next change ("both basically empty",
23G). **Design rule for re-use: three tubes per cage at 23G, or sub-24 h
replacement.**

Mortality was 5 deaths in 270 bees over two days (1.9%) against 12 in 210 over three
days (5.7%) on the 2 mL design; per bee-day, 0.9% and 1.9%. The observation windows
differ.

**What we learnt.** At the bench: air bubbles often formed while filling tubes, and
trying to remove one usually made it worse, so emptying and refilling from scratch
was more reliable. Forcing a new tube into a crowded cage during changeovers caused
major spillage, so affected bees were moved to a fresh cage instead. One or two bees
would die within 24 h, up to 4 over 48 h, which lowers the volume fed that day; we
corrected for it by adding the previous day's mean intake per bee multiplied by the
number that had died.

Above all, the volume of dsRNA needed to fill 2 mL tubes at our target concentration
was too high to make by IVT for every cage, which is why the feeders were scaled
down. The smaller feeder fixed the waste but **not the denominator**: cage feeding
cannot tell us how much each bee consumed. Intake has been reported to vary roughly
8.8-fold between bees sharing a cage `[LIT]`, and 25–45% of ingested sugar is
redistributed by trophallaxis within 48 h. Without a known dose per bee we could not
test dsRNA stability in the bee or measure how much dsRNA reaches the haemolymph.

**What it changed.** The salt result cleared our dsRNA stock buffer for every later
feeding experiment, and the tilted 23G PCR-tube feeder became the standard cage
feeder ([hardware](/hardware)). Because the project was heading towards
characterising dsRNA persistence in adult haemolymph, we needed to control the volume
each bee drank, and moved to individual harnessed feeding:
[cycle B2](/engineering#cycle-b2).
