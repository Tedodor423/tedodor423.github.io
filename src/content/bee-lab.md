Delivery is where most field RNAi work comes apart. This page is about the
biological chain that has to hold, from a patty in a hive to a mite feeding on
a bee, and the assays we had to build because they did not already exist.

## Why bees

The delivery chain, stated once and clearly: the formulation goes to the
colony, nurse bees consume it, the material reaches larvae and haemolymph, and
the mite feeds from there. Each link is a place the intervention can fail, and
each is a thing we had to be able to measure.

> **FIGURE: delivery chain.** Formulation → nurse bee → larva → haemolymph →
> mite, with the points we could measure marked and the points we could not
> marked differently. The honesty of that second category is the point of the
> figure.

## The hive insert

The physical end of the delivery chain: a 3D-printed insert that mounts inside a
standard hive frame and presents the formulation to the colony without asking
the beekeeper to learn a new procedure. It exists as CAD and as renders.

## Adult bee assays

Feeding, uptake, and recovery from adult material. What we could deliver
reliably, what varied, and what that variation meant for dose.

## Larval assays

Dosing, development, and recovery from larval material. Larvae are a different

## Varroa work

What we did with the mites themselves: husbandry, survival, and what could and
could not be tested in the time available. Where the answer is "not established",
say so and mark it **Investigated** or **Proposed** rather than stretching it.

## Developing the assay system

The methods work, the part of this project that most resembles real research.
Feeders that wasted material and the ones that did not; identifying the right
larval stage; getting haemolymph out of something small and soft; which
approaches were abandoned and why.

This section is the main source for the handbook in
