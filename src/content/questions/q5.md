## Summary

A treatment can be scientifically effective and **still fail if the people it was
designed for cannot realistically use it**. For NECTAR to make an impact, we
needed to understand adoption from the beekeeper’s perspective: not only whether
a treatment kills Varroa, but whether it is affordable, scalable, long-lasting
and compatible with existing hive-management practices. Beekeeper and industry
feedback directly shaped our pollen-patty delivery system, while colony and
economic modelling allowed us to determine appropriate treatment schedules and
evaluate whether NECTAR could provide genuine financial value. Under our current
assumptions, **our models predict a net economic benefit for treated beekeepers in
both the US and Australia**, demonstrating that designing around beekeeper needs
could make NECTAR not only practical to use, but economically worthwhile.

## Hear

Creating our project involved taking many scientific, logistical, and safety
considerations into account, but there was one other very important group of
stakeholders to talk to: beekeepers. Beyond just creating a product that works,
**we needed to create a product that beekeepers would feasibly use**; it was
incredibly important to us that their opinions guided our project development
because they were the ones we were creating it for. In order to make our project
truly effective, we reached out to beekeepers from around the world to get a
sense of what they considered when looking for treatment products, what problems
they faced with current treatments, and what they needed in a product.

## Investigate

We looked into modelling the economic burden placed on beekeepers by Varroa and
current Varroa treatments. In an operation peaking at 8,500 colonies, capital
recovery accounts for approximately 27% of total costs and labour for 21%, while
**purchased Varroa-control products alone account for approximately 9%**. This
treatment figure does not include the labour required for administration or
costs associated with rebuilding colonies lost to Varroa. Colony losses also
reduce potential income. Fewer surviving colonies means fewer hives available for
pollination contracts and honey production, while rebuilding colonies requires
additional queens, colony splits, and labour. In California, these pressures are
particularly significant because almond pollination represents a major source of
commercial beekeeper revenue. In 2024, almond pollination generated
approximately $325.8 million, representing around 81% of US pollination-service
revenue. These figures suggested that **the true cost of a Varroa treatment cannot
be measured by its purchase price alone**. For a Varroa treatment to be
effective, it must be affordable and practical at the scale at which beekeepers
operate.

## Verdict

Our conversations showed us that biological efficacy alone would not make a
treatment usable. Wade Ford identified affordability as one of the most important
considerations for Australian beekeepers, while Mike Allerton described how
rising Varroa-management costs were already pushing recreational beekeepers
towards cheaper alternatives. Comvita similarly highlighted cost as a major
determinant of adoption. Agricultural economist Brittney Goodrich helped us see
that the burden extends beyond the purchase price: labour, repeated
administration, colony replacement and lost productivity all contribute to the
true cost of treatment. Chris Hiatt, whose family manages approximately 18,000
colonies, emphasized an important point: **an effective treatment must be
scalable**. When managing thousands of hives, it is simply not feasible to
continuously visit each hive and apply a complex, labor-intensive treatment
regime. Hiatt, along with Wade Ford, Josette Lewis, and Mike Allerton, helped us
**pinpoint pollen patties as a very feasible delivery method**. These stakeholders
confirmed that many beekeepers already routinely use pollen patties to provide
their bees with supplements, and Hiatt and Lewis confirmed they would be very
scalable for commercial operations. Incorporating our treatment into something
already a part of many beekeepers’ routines would allow beekeepers to deliver
treatment without requiring a new management procedure or additional labor. Our
stakeholders helped us realize that in order to help beekeepers, **we shouldn’t
ask them to adapt their practices to NECTAR but should instead design NECTAR
around the beekeeper**.

## Evaluate

Having identified cost and scalability as major determinants of beekeeper
adoption, we evaluated NECTAR using economic models for the US and Australia
alongside DBTL Cycle 3 of our BEEHAVE model. By modelling dsRNA transfer from
pollen patty to bee to Varroa mite and varying dsRNA concentration, yeast
quantity and treatment schedule, we could estimate the amount and timing of
treatment required and compare its cost with existing approaches. Under our
current assumptions, **NECTAR produces an estimated net private benefit of
US$47.9 per treated colony per year in the US and A$18.9 per treated hive in
Australia**. Increased colony survival could also generate 8.8 million additional
pounds of honey and 123,000 pollination-capable colonies in the US, and 4.5
million additional kilograms of honey and 29,000 pollination-capable colonies in
Australia, corresponding to approximately US$4.57 million and A$4.61 million in
annual market surplus, respectively. These results allow us to approach the
question **“Would NECTAR save more money than it costs?” with a hopeful yes**.
