## Summary

Amongst all of the scientific and safety considerations remained one important
question: **who were we designing this for?** After all, a treatment can be
scientifically effective and still fail if it is not designed for the people who
are meant to use it. For NECTAR to make an impact, we needed to understand
adoption from the beekeeper’s perspective: not only whether a treatment kills
Varroa, but whether it is affordable, scalable, long-lasting and compatible with
existing hive-management practices. It was extremely important for us to
**prioritize the needs of beekeepers working on massive scales**; they are the
ones struggling to maintain their thousands of hives while dealing with
increasing colony loss from Varroa. Talking to beekeepers and the industry
directly shaped our pollen-patty delivery system, while colony and economic
modelling allowed us to determine appropriate treatment schedules and evaluate
whether NECTAR could provide genuine financial value. Under our current
assumptions, **our models predict a net economic benefit for treated beekeepers in
both the US and Australia**, demonstrating that designing around beekeeper needs
could make NECTAR not only practical to use, but economically worthwhile.

## Hear

Creating our project involved taking many scientific, logistical, and safety
considerations into account, but there was one other very important group of
stakeholders to talk to: beekeepers. Our conversations showed us that
**biological efficacy alone would not make a treatment usable**. Wade Ford
identified affordability as one of the most important considerations for
Australian beekeepers, while Mike Allerton described how rising
Varroa-management costs were already pushing recreational beekeepers towards
cheaper alternatives. Comvita similarly highlighted cost as a major determinant
of adoption. Agricultural economist Brittney Goodrich helped us see that the
burden extends beyond the purchase price: labour, repeated administration,
colony replacement and lost productivity all contribute to the true cost of
treatment. Chris Hiatt, whose family manages approximately 18,000 colonies,
emphasized an important point: **an effective treatment must be scalable**. When
managing thousands of hives, it is simply not feasible to continuously visit
each hive and apply a complex, labor-intensive treatment regime. Hiatt, along
with Wade Ford, Josette Lewis, and Mike Allerton, helped us **pinpoint pollen
patties as a very feasible delivery method**. These stakeholders confirmed that
many beekeepers already routinely use pollen patties to provide their bees with
supplements, and Hiatt and Lewis confirmed they would be very scalable for
commercial operations. It was extremely important for us to hear this feedback
and take it into account when designing our product.

## Investigate

We investigated both the economic and practical constraints surrounding Varroa
management to understand what would make NECTAR viable for commercial
beekeepers. In Brittney Goodrich’s representative operation with 8,500
colonies, capital recovery accounts for approximately 27% of total costs, labour
21%, and **Varroa-control products alone 9%, excluding treatment labour and
colony-replacement costs**; this expensive cost is further amplified as colony
losses also reduce income from honey and valuable pollination contracts, with
California almond pollination alone generating approximately $325.8 million in
2024 and accounting for 81% of all US pollination-service revenue (1). We
therefore used our BEEHAVE model to investigate whether treatment timing could
reduce this burden, finding that **winter-only treatment could achieve comparable
efficacy to more frequent treatment schedules**, potentially reducing both
product use and beekeeper labour. However, this revealed an important practical
conflict: pollen patties are more commonly associated with periods of protein
demand such as spring build-up, while opening hives repeatedly during cold
winter conditions can disturb the cluster and risk colony stress. Our
investigation therefore showed that **affordability depends not only on treatment
price, but also on when and how NECTAR is administered**, highlighting the need
to reconcile our modelled optimal treatment schedule with real-world seasonal
beekeeping practices.

## Verdict

Talking with key beekeepers and industry stakeholders made us realize how
important it was to design our product around the people who needed it most.
Our stakeholders helped us realize that in order to help beekeepers, **we
shouldn’t ask them to adapt their practices to NECTAR but should instead design
NECTAR around the beekeeper**. Rather than designing a new application procedure
and asking the beekeepers to change their practices, we decided to incorporate
our design into something they already used. Incorporating our treatment into
something already a part of many beekeepers’ routines would allow beekeepers to
deliver treatment without requiring a new management procedure or additional
labor. We couldn’t just create a product that worked; we needed to create **a
product that the people we were creating it for could feasibly use**. This meant
**choosing pollen patties as our delivery method** and doing experimental testing
and modeling to ensure the amount of dsRNA in yeast would keep the product
affordable.

## Evaluate

Having identified cost and scalability as major determinants of beekeeper
adoption, we evaluated NECTAR using economic models for the US and Australia
alongside DBTL Cycle 3 of our BEEHAVE model. By modelling dsRNA transfer from
pollen patty to bee to Varroa mite and varying dsRNA concentration, yeast
quantity and treatment schedule, we could estimate the amount and timing of
treatment required and compare its cost with existing approaches. Under our
current assumptions, **NECTAR produces an estimated net private benefit of
US$47.9 per treated colony per year in the US and A$18.9 per treated hive in
Australia**. Increased colony survival could also generate 8.8 million additional
pounds of honey and 123,000 pollination-capable colonies in the US, and 4.5
million additional kilograms of honey and 29,000 pollination-capable colonies in
Australia, corresponding to approximately US$4.57 million and A$4.61 million in
annual market surplus, respectively. These results allow us to approach the
question **“Would NECTAR save more money than it costs?” with a hopeful yes**.
