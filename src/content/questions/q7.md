---
title: How would NECTAR impact wider economies?
hear: thurman*
---

## Hear

We began by defining what economic benefit should mean for NECTAR. Wally
Thurman's advice was to start at the level of an average commercial beekeeper,
comparing NECTAR against existing treatments in terms of costs and revenues
before scaling up to wider market effects. That complemented what we heard from
beekeepers: Varroa creates costs not only through treatment purchases, but
through colony replacement, labour, repeated hive visits and treatment failure.
So colony survival, labour and treatment expenditure became economic variables
rather than only biological ones.

## Observe

Australia has around 530,000 commercial managed hives, with Varroa already
adding direct treatment and labour costs, while pollination supports a much
wider agricultural economy. The US system is larger and more commercialised:
about 2.412 million honey-producing colonies and major pollination markets such
as almonds, but high colony losses and recurring control costs with them.
Improved colony survival could cut beekeeper costs while preserving honey
production and the supply of colonies available for paid pollination.

## Navigate

We designed the analysis around NECTAR's incremental value against realistic
alternatives, rather than claiming a share of the total value of
pollination-dependent agriculture. At beekeeper level we modelled avoided colony
replacement, reduced Varroa spending, labour savings and the cost of NECTAR
itself, then extended that into a simple partial-equilibrium model running from
treated colonies to fewer losses, more surviving productive colonies, greater
honey and pollination supply, and changes in prices and surplus. The US and
Australia got separate models rather than one global scenario.

## Evaluate

Under the scenarios tested, the models estimate a net private benefit of about
US$47.9 per treated colony per year in the US and A$18.9 per treated hive in
Australia, mainly through lower replacement, treatment and labour costs. At
market level, about 8.8 million lb more honey and 123,000 more
pollination-capable colonies in the US, and 4.5 million kg and 29,000 colonies
in Australia: roughly US$4.57 million and A$4.61 million a year in modelled
market surplus. Those benefits are shared across beekeepers, honey buyers,
growers and consumers rather than captured by NECTAR.

## Yield

Protecting a colony has value beyond keeping one hive alive, so improvements at
hive level scale into wider agricultural markets. The modelling also gives
engineering targets: treatment cost, dosing frequency, labour requirement and
colony-survival benefit all have to be good enough for the product to remain
economically worthwhile.
