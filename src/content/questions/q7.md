## Summary

**The economic cost of Varroa extends far beyond the price of treatment**: colony
losses mean replacement costs, lost honey, reduced pollination capacity and
additional labour. We therefore modelled whether NECTAR could generate measurable
economic value for both individual beekeepers and the wider agricultural economy.
Our results were promising: under the scenarios tested, **NECTAR produced a
positive net private benefit per treated hive in both the US and Australia**,
while improved colony survival generated additional honey and pollination
capacity and increased modelled market surplus. These findings suggest that
NECTAR could not only protect bees, but also save beekeepers money and create
wider economic benefits for pollination-dependent agriculture.

## Hear

We began by defining what economic benefit should mean for NECTAR. The interview
with Professor Wally Thurman inspired us to **start at the level of an average
commercial beekeeper**, comparing NECTAR against existing treatments in terms of
costs and revenues before scaling up to wider market effects. This complemented
what we heard from beekeepers: Varroa creates costs not only through treatment
purchases, but through colony replacement, labour, repeated hive visits and
treatment failure. As a result, **we treated colony survival, labour and
treatment expenditure as key economic variables** rather than considering Varroa
only as a biological problem.

## Investigate

We examined the honey and pollination economies of Australia and the United
States to understand where NECTAR could create value. Australia has around
530,000 commercial managed hives, with Varroa already adding direct treatment and
labour costs, while pollination supports a much wider agricultural economy. The
US has a larger and more commercialised system, with approximately 2.412 million
honey-producing colonies and major pollination markets such as almonds, but it
also faces high colony losses and recurring Varroa-control costs. Together, these
markets showed that **improved colony survival could reduce beekeeper costs while
also preserving honey production and the supply of colonies available for paid
pollination**.

## Verdict

Our current modelling suggests that **NECTAR could create positive economic value
at both the beekeeper and market level** under the scenarios tested. The models
estimate a net private benefit of approximately US$47.9 per treated colony per
year in the US, and A$18.9 per treated hive per year in Australia, mainly through
lower replacement, treatment and labour costs. When extended to the wider market,
improved colony survival could add around 8.8 million lb of honey and 123,000
pollination-capable colonies in the US, and around 4.5 million kg of honey and
29,000 pollination-capable colonies in Australia, corresponding to roughly
US$4.57 million per year and A$4.61 million respectively in modelled market
surplus. **These benefits would be shared across beekeepers, honey buyers, growers
and potentially consumers** rather than being captured entirely by NECTAR itself.

## Evaluate

Our economic analysis showed that **protecting a colony has value far beyond
keeping a single hive alive**. A surviving colony continues to produce honey, can
provide pollination services and avoids the costs of replacement, meaning that
improvements at the hive level can scale into wider agricultural markets. Under
our current scenarios, NECTAR could therefore support both beekeeper profitability
and broader consumer and producer surpluses in honey and pollination markets. The
modelling also gives us **practical engineering targets including lowering
treatment cost, dosing frequency, and labor requirements**.
