## Summary

Before diving in to creating a new treatment, we needed to understand the scale
of the Varroa problem and determine what was wrong with current treatments.
Through research, modelling and conversations with beekeepers and bee-health
experts across the UK, US and Australia, we found that **Varroa management is
constrained by more than treatment efficacy alone**. Resistance, reinfestation,
climate-dependent treatments, residues, repeated application, labour, and cost
can all limit existing approaches, while uncontrolled Varroa contributes to viral
disease, colony loss and wider economic impacts on pollination-dependent
agriculture. These findings changed our definition of a successful treatment:
rather than simply asking whether NECTAR could kill mites, **we asked whether it
could provide safe, practical and sustained Varroa control while reducing the
likelihood of resistance and the burden placed on beekeepers.**

## Hear

*Varroa destructor* is one of the most damaging honeybee parasites. It feeds on
both adult and larval bees, weakening their immune system. Varroa also acts as a
vector for several bee viruses, mainly deformed wing virus and the acute bee
paralysis virus complex (1). It causes deaths of millions of honeybee colonies
worldwide, leading to losses of billions of dollars in agricultural production
(2). **Most of the honeybee colonies infested with varroa collapse within 2–3
years without periodic treatment** (3). To better understand the current
beekeeping practices and gain insight into available treatments, we reached out
to experts, stakeholders, and those directly affected by varroa.

## Investigate

Currently, there exists a range of different treatment methods targeting Varroa,
including synthetic miticides, organic acids and essential oils, and mechanical
and cultural controls. Some widely used synthetic miticides include pyrethroids,
coumaphos, and amitraz, but **many of these treatments have succumbed to the
development of resistance**. For example, mites resistant to pyrethroid-based
treatments were first detected in Italy in 1991 (4). During the following years,
the resistant mite started to spread across Europe, reaching the UK in August
2001 (5). The resistant mites also spread to countries outside of Europe; they
were first observed in the US in 1997 (5). As time has progressed, mites have
been observed as becoming resistant to multiple treatments at once (6). However,
as even more treatment is applied to combat re-infestation, some of the
synthetic miticides have accumulated in bee brood, beeswax, or honey, which can
harm both human and honeybee health; accumulation of coumaphos was observed in
bee brood and beeswax (7). Other treatments face similar difficulties: organic
acids (oxalic and formic acid) face off-label use, which can potentially pose a
harm to people or the environment. Formic acid is also not suitable for hot
climates (8), which means that it cannot be used in certain regions. Some
essential oils, such as thymol, may affect the taste of the honey (9). Most
common mechanical and cultural controls, including drone brood removal, brood
interruption, and screened bottom boards, require very high labour intensity and
suffer from low standalone efficacy.

## Verdict

The first step in designing an effective solution was understanding Varroa not
only as a biological problem, but as an ecological, economic and practical
challenge. Stakeholders across the beekeeping and pollination industries
described limitations of existing treatments, including rapid reinfestation,
emerging chemical resistance, climate restrictions, off-label use and the
substantial cost and labour of repeated treatment. Danny Le Feuvre described
Australian beekeepers returning to treatment thresholds within weeks, while
Elizabeth Frost highlighted emerging resistance, Wade Ford identified limitations
of organic-acid treatments, and Mike Allerton emphasised the burden of travelling
long distances to treat apiaries. In California, Josette Lewis showed us how
colony losses can have consequences beyond beekeepers for pollination-dependent
industries such as almonds. Together, these conversations **changed our question
from “How can we kill Varroa?” to “How can we reduce Varroa’s wider burden?”**
They established that an effective treatment must combine mite control with
affordability, low labour requirements, bee and human safety, and compatibility
with agriculture, while **concerns about resistance encouraged us to explore
multiple RNA targets** to reduce the likelihood of resistance developing.

## Evaluate

To evaluate whether NECTAR could overcome the limitations of existing Varroa
treatments, **we expanded our BEEHAVE model to simulate NECTAR directly**. In our
third DBTL cycle, we modelled the pathway from a bee consuming dsRNA-containing
pollen patties, to dsRNA reaching a phoretic mite, to that exposure causing mite
death. We then varied dsRNA concentration (0.0005–0.05), yeast consumption
(0.001–0.1 g per bee per day), and treatment timing, comparing year-round,
autumn and winter treatment schedules at an initial Varroa infestation level of
10%. This allowed us to evaluate **which parameters most strongly determine
Varroa mortality** and whether increasing dsRNA concentration or simply
increasing the amount of treated pollen patty would be the more effective
intervention.
