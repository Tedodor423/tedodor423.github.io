## Summary

Designing a treatment to kill Varroa also meant **taking responsibility for
everything we did not intend to kill**. While beekeepers primarily emphasised
efficacy, affordability and labour, our conversations with regulators and
environmental scientists highlighted another essential requirement: ecological
safety. This pushed us to trace where our dsRNA could travel, consider which
non-target organisms might encounter it, and integrate off-target screening
directly into our RNA-design pipeline. Rather than treating safety as something
to prove after developing NECTAR, **we made specificity and environmental impact
part of how we designed the treatment from the beginning.**

## Hear

As we developed a Varroa-specific treatment, we needed to ask whether killing
our target was enough. Interestingly, **ecological safety emerged much more
strongly in our conversations with academics and regulators than with
beekeepers**, whose priorities centred largely on efficacy, cost and labour.
Professor Paul Lam identified off-target effects as one of the key environmental
risks of an RNAi biopesticide and encouraged us to screen representative
non-target species. The Australian OGTR highlighted native bees, mites and other
insects that could encounter our treatment, while Lord John Krebs encouraged us
to consider organisms closely related to Varroa and also investigate any dsRNA
entry into honey. Jim Dunwell, Chair of ACRE, framed the issue particularly
clearly: regulators would follow potential **“pathways to harm”**, asking where
our therapeutic could travel, what could encounter it, and what would happen
following exposure. Luckily, researcher Dr. Theotime Colin assured us that **he
wasn’t worried about any off-target effects**, as the Varroa mite is very unique
and he doesn’t think there will be any off-target impacts on other arthropods.

## Investigate

Our regulatory research showed that **moving from living *S. alvi* to
heat-inactivated yeast would not eliminate the need to demonstrate ecological
safety**. Even without releasing a living GMO, the dsRNA itself could potentially
move beyond the intended Varroa target, meaning regulators could still require
evidence addressing bee safety, environmental exposure, non-target organisms,
and possible entry into honey. This shifted our focus from asking simply whether
the delivery organism was contained to tracing the whole pathway of the
therapeutic after administration. RNAi's sequence specificity offered an
opportunity to design against these risks, but specificity could not simply be
assumed. This was particularly important because regulators told us that **the
burden would ultimately be on us to provide sufficient evidence of safety**.

## Verdict

We therefore **made ecological safety a criterion during RNA design** rather than
something to test only after selecting our most effective target; this meant
screening at the time of design and incorporating that into our RNA design
platform rather than screening after choosing the most effective target.
Following Paul Lam's advice, our NectarDesigner pipeline incorporated
bioinformatic off-target screening using BLAST against representative
non-target species, allowing candidate dsRNA sequences with concerning sequence
similarity to be deprioritised. We also incorporated the honey bee itself into
our screening and considered organisms with greater biological relevance to
Varroa exposure. Alongside sequence design, concerns raised by regulators and
the honey industry led us to investigate dsRNA persistence and degradation in
honey, broadening safety from **“will this harm the bee?” to “where could the
therapeutic go, and what could it affect?”** Even though off-target effects are
biologically improbable, policy reminds us that **it is still extremely important
to design and test our product thoroughly with safety in mind**.

## Evaluate

Analyzing BLAST similarity will hopefully have allowed us to reduce off-target
risks in representative species at the design stage, but **it is a prediction
rather than a result**; in future development stages, experimental testing
should be done in representative non-target organisms and in honey to
demonstrate ecological safety. Testing must also be done to determine what
happens in instances of misuse or when there is leftover product; at this
stage, **we simply don’t know the shelf-life, ideal disposal methods, or how long
dsRNA can last in unused products**. In the future, it will be crucial to ensure
testing is done to guarantee that **NECTAR achieves target specificity and is
truly ecologically safe**.
