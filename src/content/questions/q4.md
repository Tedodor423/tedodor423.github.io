## Hear

Off-target harm was raised by academics and regulators — Lam on
ecotoxicology, Dunwell and the OGTR on non-target mites, native bees and
spread, Krebs on other arachnids and on allergy if anything reaches honey,
the US agencies on bee, user, food and environmental safety. It came up far
less with beekeepers, whose first concern was almost always cost. That
mismatch is a finding, not an aside.

## Observe

Regulators care about off-target effects even if it is not a live GMO
supplying the dsRNA. Dropping the living chassis removed a category of
questions about the organism and none about the molecule: where the dsRNA goes, and what it
silences when it gets there.

## Navigate

Off-target screening was built into target selection rather than run as a
check afterwards, and the honey pathway became a wet-lab question: residues,
and whether treatment perturbs the NMR and LC-HRMS purity tests — including
after the 65 °C processing Ford described.

## Evaluate

We can state the design intent and the screening method; we cannot yet state
a result. No off-target effects would mean substantially less ecological
impact at comparable efficacy and cost — a target, not a finding.

## Yield

Input from the academic and regulatory side, not from the market, reoriented
target design towards ecological safety alongside efficacy. Next question: a
product safe enough to approve is still no use if nobody will buy it.
