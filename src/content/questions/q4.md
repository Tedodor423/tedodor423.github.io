## Summary

Designing a treatment to kill Varroa also meant **taking responsibility for
everything we did not intend to kill**. While beekeepers primarily emphasised
efficacy, affordability and labour, our conversations with regulators and
environmental scientists highlighted another essential requirement: ecological
safety. This pushed us to trace where our dsRNA could travel, consider which
non-target organisms might encounter it, and integrate off-target screening
directly into our RNA-design pipeline. Rather than treating safety as something
to prove after developing NECTAR, **we made specificity and environmental impact
part of how we designed the treatment from the beginning.**

## Hear

As we developed a Varroa-specific treatment, we needed to ask whether killing
our target was enough. Interestingly, **ecological safety emerged much more
strongly in our conversations with academics and regulators than with
beekeepers**, whose priorities centred largely on efficacy, cost and labour.
Professor Paul Lam identified off-target effects as one of the key environmental
risks of an RNAi biopesticide and encouraged us to screen representative
non-target species. Lord John Krebs similarly encouraged us to consider
organisms closely related to Varroa, while the Australian OGTR highlighted native
bees, mites and other insects that could encounter our treatment. Jim Dunwell,
Chair of ACRE, framed the issue particularly clearly: regulators would follow
potential **“pathways to harm”**, asking where our therapeutic could travel, what
could encounter it, and what would happen following exposure.

## Investigate

Our regulatory research showed that **moving from living *S. alvi* to
heat-inactivated yeast would not eliminate the need to demonstrate ecological
safety**. Even without releasing a living GMO, the dsRNA itself could potentially
move beyond the intended Varroa target, meaning regulators could still require
evidence addressing bee safety, environmental exposure, non-target organisms and
possible entry into honey. This shifted our focus from asking simply whether the
delivery organism was contained to tracing the whole pathway of the therapeutic
after administration. RNAi's sequence specificity offered an opportunity to
design against these risks, but specificity could not simply be assumed. This
was particularly important because regulators told us that **the burden would
ultimately be on us to provide sufficient evidence of safety**.

## Verdict

We therefore **made ecological safety a criterion during RNA design** rather than
something to test only after selecting our most effective target. Following Paul
Lam's advice, our NectarDesigner pipeline incorporated bioinformatic off-target
screening using BLAST against representative non-target species, allowing
candidate dsRNA sequences with concerning sequence similarity to be
deprioritised. We also incorporated the honey bee itself into our screening and
considered organisms with greater biological relevance to Varroa exposure.
Alongside sequence design, concerns raised by regulators and the honey industry
led us to investigate dsRNA persistence and degradation in honey, broadening
safety from **“will this harm the bee?” to “where could the therapeutic go, and
what could it affect?”**

## Evaluate

Our work allowed us to **reduce predicted off-target risk at the design stage**,
and in future development, experimental testing can be done in representative
non-target organisms to demonstrate ecological safety. NECTAR must aim to
achieve target specificity and ecological safety **without making treatment
prohibitively expensive for the people expected to use it**.
