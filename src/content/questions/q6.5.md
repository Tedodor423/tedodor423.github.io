## Summary

Varroa is a global problem, but **there is no universal beekeeping environment**.
Differences in infestation history, treatment resistance, industry scale,
regulation, economics, and beekeeper practices mean that a treatment suitable
for one region may be impractical—or have very different value—in another. We
therefore compared Australia, the US and UK through stakeholder engagement,
regulatory research, regional case studies and economic modelling to determine
the most practical and economically viable solution for around the world. This
helped us **design NECTAR as an adaptable treatment rather than one built around a
single country**, while allowing us to quantify whether protecting colonies could
create economic value for beekeepers and the wider agricultural systems that
depend upon them.

## Hear

We spoke with stakeholders across the UK, US and Australia to understand **how
both the requirements and value of NECTAR might change between regions**. Danny
Le Feuvre and Wade Ford described an Australian industry newly adapting to
Varroa, where resistance, reinfestation and treatment costs are creating
substantial pressure. In California, Josette Lewis and Chris Hiatt demonstrated
the very different needs for a highly commercialised, migratory pollination
industry operating across thousands of colonies. Regulators across all three
countries also showed us that **the same biotechnology could encounter very
different approval pathways**. To understand how these differences translated
into economic value, Professor Wally Thurman encouraged us to **begin at the level
of an average commercial beekeeper**—comparing NECTAR with existing treatment,
labour and colony-replacement costs—before scaling up to wider honey and
pollination markets.

## Investigate

Our research confirmed substantial differences between regions. Australia only
detected Varroa in 2022 and abandoned eradication in 2023, while California's
established Varroa problem exists within a system where approximately 1.5–2
million colonies are transported annually for almond pollination (1).
Regulation varied just as significantly: living engineered microorganisms can
trigger additional GMO release and containment requirements in some
jurisdictions, whereas other regulatory systems focus more strongly on the
product's function and risk. The economics also differed; our datasets included
approximately 530,000 commercial managed hives in Australia compared with
approximately 2.412 million honey-producing colonies in the US, where honey
production and major paid pollination markets such as almonds make colony
survival particularly economically valuable. Together, this showed us that
**although the biological problem is global, the practical, regulatory and
economic conditions governing an acceptable treatment are local**.

## Verdict

We therefore designed NECTAR around **features that could translate between these
environments** while recognising that implementation would need to remain
adaptable. Australian regulatory and beekeeper perspectives highlighted a
trade-off between the persistence of living *S. alvi* and the greater
deployability of a non-living formulation; Californian stakeholders emphasised
scalability across thousands of colonies; UK stakeholders highlighted
environmental safety, food regulation, and public acceptance. Together, these
perspectives strengthened our decision to **prioritise heat-inactivated yeast
delivered through pollen patties**, combining a more containable formulation with
a delivery practice already familiar to beekeepers internationally. Following
Wally Thurman's advice, we also **developed separate US and Australian economic
models** rather than assuming NECTAR would have the same value everywhere,
comparing its incremental benefits against realistic existing Varroa-management
practices in each market.

## Evaluate

Our modelling suggests that **NECTAR could create positive economic value in both
regions** despite their very different beekeeping systems. Under our current
assumptions, we estimate a net private benefit of approximately US$47.9 per
treated colony per year in the US and A$18.9 per treated hive per year in
Australia, primarily through reduced treatment, labour and colony-replacement
costs. At market level, improved colony survival could add approximately 8.8
million lb of honey and 123,000 pollination-capable colonies in the US,
corresponding to around US$4.57 million/year in modelled market surplus. In
Australia, we estimate approximately 4.5 million kg of additional honey and
29,000 additional pollination-capable colonies, corresponding to around A$4.61
million/year. Protecting a colony therefore has value beyond keeping one hive
alive: it preserves honey production, pollination capacity and beekeeper
income. Importantly, the different results between countries reinforced rather
than undermined our conclusion: **NECTAR does not need to have identical value
everywhere to create meaningful value everywhere**.
