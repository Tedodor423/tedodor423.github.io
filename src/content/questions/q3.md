## Summary

Our original vision for NECTAR centred on engineered *S. alvi* living inside the
honey bee and continuously producing therapeutic dsRNA. However, conversations
with beekeepers, industry representatives and regulators challenged us to
consider **whether biological performance alone justified releasing a
self-replicating engineered organism**. Comparing *S. alvi* with an inactivated
yeast system revealed trade-offs between persistence and practicality, including
regulation, containment, environmental spread, public acceptance, manufacturing
and compatibility with existing beekeeping practices. These considerations
ultimately drove one of the largest pivots in our project: **from a persistent
living GMO towards engineered yeast that produces dsRNA before being
heat-inactivated and delivered through familiar pollen patties**, allowing us to
retain the benefits of synthetic biology while designing for responsible
real-world deployment.

## Hear

We began by listening to beekeepers, industry leaders and honey businesses to
understand what a useful Varroa treatment would actually need to achieve. Their
feedback showed that **efficacy alone was not enough**: cost, labour, frequency of
application, compatibility with existing practices, residues in honey, resistance
and public acceptance all mattered. While some stakeholders valued *S. alvi*
because its persistence could reduce repeated treatment, others preferred pollen
patties and a non-living formulation because these fitted more easily into
existing workflows and avoided concerns around living GMOs. Discussions with
leading researchers in the field including Leonard, Barrick and Moran shaped our
exploration of *S. alvi* and highlighted the regulatory challenges of a living
GMO, while Wright’s advice helped steer us towards heat-inactivated yeast
delivered through pollen patties as a more practical alternative.

## Investigate

We then examined the wider regulatory, environmental and commercial context
surrounding potential dsRNA production and delivery strategies. We compared
*S. alvi*, *E. coli* and *S. cerevisiae*, ultimately **selecting yeast because
dsRNA could accumulate and remain intact, while heat-inactivated cells could be
incorporated directly into pollen patties** without costly downstream RNA
purification or formulation. This also offered advantages over a living
engineered *S. alvi* system which raised additional regulatory concerns
associated with deploying a live GMO. Together, these considerations pushed us
towards heat-inactivated yeast as a production and delivery strategy that
balanced biological performance with scalability, practicality and regulatory
feasibility.

## Verdict

Using what we learned, **we changed the product itself**. Rather than relying on a
living bacterium to persist inside bees, we shifted towards using engineered
*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* to produce dsRNA, followed by heat inactivation and
formulation into a non-living product. Yeast offered mature fermentation
infrastructure, established engineering tools, favourable dsRNA accumulation and
the possibility of avoiding expensive purification steps. To compensate for the
loss of biological persistence, **we designed the treatment around pollen patties
already used by beekeepers**, allowing repeated dosing to fit into existing
hive-management practices.

## Evaluate

We moved from a living, self-replicating *S. alvi* system towards a
**yeast-produced, non-living dsRNA formulation** delivered through an established
hive-feeding method. This approach keeps the advantages of synthetic biology for
controlled dsRNA production while avoiding the need for an engineered organism
to persist in the environment. Our final design therefore reflects not just what
could work biologically, but **what could also be contained, regulated,
manufactured, accepted and realistically used by beekeepers**.
