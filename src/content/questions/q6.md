## Summary

Varroa may be a global problem, but **there is no universal beekeeping
environment**. Differences in infestation history, treatment resistance, industry
scale, beekeeper practices, regulation and economics mean that a treatment
suitable for one region may be impractical in another. By comparing Australia,
the US and UK through stakeholder engagement, regulatory research, case studies
and economic modelling, we asked what NECTAR would need to become a globally
adaptable solution rather than one designed around a single country. This
ultimately shaped both **our choice of a more deployable non-living formulation
and a delivery method compatible with existing beekeeping practices**, while
demonstrating that NECTAR could provide value across very different agricultural
systems.

## Hear

The world is a big place – our project aim was to create a product that
successfully kills Varroa, but we quickly realized a big problem: **different
regions around the world were struggling with Varroa in different ways**. In order
to create a solution that could be applied universally, we needed to do a
deep-dive into the different considerations and contexts of various regions to
better understand what they needed. To do this, we reached out to stakeholders
across the UK, the US, and Australia to get a sense of the different strategies
currently being used to combat Varroa and how we could design something that
would work for everyone.

## Investigate

Varroa is a global problem, but the conditions surrounding it vary dramatically.
Australia only detected Varroa in 2022 and abandoned eradication in 2023; while
strict border controls have kept parts of western Australia largely Varroa-free,
eastern regions now face widespread infestation, treatment resistance and high
costs, particularly among recreational beekeepers. California presents a very
different challenge: approximately 1.5–2 million colonies are transported across
the US each year to pollinate almonds, creating a highly industrialised system in
which Varroa management must operate at enormous scale.

Regulation varies just as substantially: our mapping across Australia, Europe,
Switzerland, Singapore and the US found that living engineered microorganisms can
face additional GMO release and containment requirements, while non-living dsRNA
formulations may avoid some GMO-specific regulation but still require product
approval. Together, these differences taught us that **although the biological
problem is global, the economic, practical and regulatory conditions governing an
acceptable solution are local**—meaning NECTAR must be adaptable to different
real-world contexts.

## Verdict

Our conversations confirmed that different regions create different priorities
for NECTAR. In Australia, Danny Le Feuvre and Wade Ford described reinfestation,
treatment resistance, cost and frequent reapplication as major challenges,
making long-lasting protection desirable. However, the Office of the Gene
Technology Regulator confirmed that living engineered *S. alvi* would fall within
its regulatory remit, while our non-living yeast formulation would not—**creating
a trade-off between persistence and deployability**. In California, Josette Lewis
and Chris Hiatt emphasised the need for a treatment scalable across an enormous
migratory commercial beekeeping industry, while US regulators indicated that GMO
status itself would not necessarily create the same barrier. In the UK, Jim
Dunwell emphasised environmental outcomes over delivery method, while Lord Krebs
highlighted potential food-regulation and public-acceptance challenges
surrounding GMOs. Together, these perspectives encouraged us to **prioritise the
non-living yeast route and pollen-patty delivery**, balancing regulatory
accessibility with a familiar and scalable beekeeping practice. Examining these
regional differences helped us design NECTAR not only for the UK, but with the
needs of beekeepers around the world in mind.

## Evaluate

To evaluate how NECTAR's impact might differ between real-world environments, we
developed separate economic models for the US and Australia. The differences were
substantial: our Australian dataset estimates approximately 530,000 commercial
managed hives, with Varroa management adding around A$22.18 per hive in direct
control costs plus 0.37 hours of additional labour, while the US has
approximately 2.412 million honey-producing colonies, with Varroa control costing
around US$9.22 per colony annually alongside a highly commercialised pollination
industry. Reflecting these different conditions, **our model estimates a net
private benefit from NECTAR of approximately US$47.9 per treated colony per year
in the US, compared with A$18.9 per treated hive per year in Australia**. We then
expanded this comparison to the wider honey and pollination markets. Under our
current assumptions, improved colony survival could produce approximately 8.8
million additional pounds of honey and 123,000 additional pollination-capable
colonies in the US, corresponding to around US$4.57 million/year in modelled
market surplus. In Australia, the equivalent model predicts approximately 4.5
million additional kilograms of honey and 29,000 additional pollination-capable
colonies, corresponding to around A$4.61 million/year. Despite these differences
in economic value, it confirmed that **the choices we made to ensure our treatment
would be usable everywhere would allow our treatment to provide substantial
financial benefits** to beekeepers and agricultural industries around the world.
