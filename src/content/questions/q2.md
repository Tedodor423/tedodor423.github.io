## Summary

Having identified the limitations of existing chemical acaricides, we asked
whether RNA interference (RNAi) could be a more effective, adaptable and
environmentally responsible alternative. Existing RNAi treatments such as Norroa
demonstrated that the approach was scientifically and regulatorily feasible. It
also revealed opportunities to improve efficacy, targeting and delivery.
Combining this precedent with stakeholder feedback, **we chose dsRNA and
redesigned our approach around direct mite mortality, off-target safety, and
rational dsRNA design**. Ultimately, RNAi offered us more than another Varroa
treatment as **its modularity and flexibility gave us the potential to develop
NECTAR as a platform for designing targeted RNAi-based interventions.**

## Hear

To decide whether we would pursue dsRNA, we consulted beekeeping,
environmental, laboratory, and regulatory perspectives. Lam outlined key
drawbacks of conventional chemical pesticides (off-target effects,
bioaccumulation, biomagnification, and resistance development), Le Feuvre and
Hiatt reported chemical miticides were failing despite combinations and
rotations, and Frost highlighted that **all legal synthetic miticides were
expected to become ineffective soon due to resistance**. Combined, these views
pointed towards the need for **a more environmentally-friendly treatment with a
distinct mode of action**, which dsRNA offers since it is biodegradable and acts
by silencing genes. Budge affirmed laboratory assays we proposed to test dsRNA
efficacy, suggesting that validation was feasible for this approach. However,
Lam warned that **off-target effects remained a key concern for dsRNA**, echoing
US Federal Regulators’ concerns about bee and human safety. To minimise this
risk, he agreed that bioinformatics tools could be used for screening them
against representative species. Finally, Frost and Colin suggested that RNAi
could be used against other threats to honey bees like the small hive beetle,
leading to our longer-term view of **NECTAR as a modular RNAi platform with
Varroa as a first case study rather than an end goal**.

## Investigate

Investigating the existing RNAi landscape, the U.S. EPA has already registered
two RNAi-based biopesticides, including Norroa for Varroa mite control
(1)(2)(3), demonstrating that **dsRNA products can meet regulatory requirements
for use in apiculture**. However, as Norroa targets calmodulin, suppressing mite
reproduction instead of killing them directly, based on results from field
trials, it is suggested to be **only useful for maintaining low mite infestation
levels rather than reducing high infestations** (4)(5). Additionally, we read
that resistance towards dsRNA can be selected experimentally in other
arthropods like the Western corn rootworm and Colorado potato beetle which have
evolved resistance towards dsRNA (6)(7), raising questions about whether Varroa
could evolve resistance towards our product. Although silencing of individual
genes in Varroa has been studied, to our knowledge, **there is no framework for
rational design of dsRNA targets against Varroa** through choosing the target
gene, the region of the target gene, and targets to multiplex for effectiveness
and lowering risk for resistance development. Altogether, these findings
suggest that the RNAi-based approach is feasible from a regulatory perspective,
and that the lack of rational design frameworks, limited efficacy at high
infestation, and vulnerability to resistance were **weaknesses that NECTAR could
improve upon**.

## Verdict

Despite concerns surrounding off-target effects and possible resistance, we
decided to proceed with the RNAi-based approach as it provided **three key
benefits: effectiveness, modularity, and biodegradability**. Its new mode of
action could kill mites resistant to conventional chemical acaricides and be
incorporated into IPM, its modularity enables multiplexing of gene targets to
combat resistance or usage against other pests, and it degrades naturally so
would not bioaccumulate or biomagnify, posing less environmental risks than
existing treatments. As we found no standardised framework for selecting dsRNA
constructs against Varroa, **we built NectarDesigner to bridge the gap**, and two
decisions were also made regarding its specifics. Firstly, because beekeepers
reported high mite infestation levels and the lack of effective treatments,
rather than targeting mite reproduction like Norroa, **we decided to additionally
target mite survival genes** to further improve efficacy. Secondly, to address
the concern about off-target effects, **we therefore added off-target analysis
against *Apis mellifera* and wider off-target analysis against related
arthropods, pollinators sharing the environment, and humans** into the final
stage of NectarDesigner.

## Evaluate

NectarDesigner, aimed at selecting dsRNA constructs which promoted both efficacy
and safety, **shortlisted five candidate genes**. The top-scoring gene,
Vd-CHIBIN, previously reported one of the strongest knockdowns by RNAi (90–99%)
associated with a significant decrease in mite survival (40–50%) (8), and we
studied the dsRNA construct targeting this gene in the lab as well as designing
multigene constructs. In principle, the gains in efficacy and safety would
better support honey bee colonies, beekeepers, and the wider ecosystem.
However, even though off-target analysis was conducted, **not all risks may have
been identified**. Since not all species were screened against, species without
transcriptomes could not be screened, and the screen relies on an exact-match of
at least 16 nucleotides, so broadly similar sequences with differences
distributed along them may not be detected. Besides, dsRNA selection alone did
not determine efficacy, targeting dsRNA to the mites was equally important,
which led to our next question: **how should we deliver NECTAR?**
