## Summary

Having identified the limitations of existing chemical acaricides, we asked
whether RNA interference could provide a more effective, adaptable and
environmentally responsible alternative. Existing RNAi treatments such as Norroa
demonstrated that the approach was scientifically and regulatorily feasible, but
also revealed opportunities to improve efficacy, targeting and delivery.
Combining this precedent with stakeholder feedback led us to **pursue dsRNA while
redesigning our approach around direct mite mortality, scalable delivery and
off-target safety**. Ultimately, RNAi offered us more than another Varroa
treatment: **its modularity and flexibility gave us the potential to develop
NECTAR as a platform for designing targeted RNAi-based therapeutics.**

## Hear

The cost and problems of chemical acaricides led to our interest in a
fundamentally different mechanism for control: **RNA interference (RNAi)**, which
from our R&D perspective, provided much room for investigating and optimising
NECTAR. To confirm whether we would pursue dsRNA rather than another chemical, we
consulted environmental, beekeeping, laboratory, and regulatory perspectives.
These conversations exposed the severe limitations of chemical acaricides,
supported our idea to pursue RNAi-based treatments, and **sparked changes to our
initial project design to prioritise effectiveness, scalability and safety**.

## Investigate

We learned that the U.S. EPA has already registered two RNAi-based
biopesticides, one of which, Norroa, controls Varroa mite populations. However,
**it requires weeks to take effect as it targets mite reproduction instead of
survival**, and targets worker bees instead of larvae which Varroa primarily feeds
upon. Realising the feasibility of regulatory approval and the potential to
further benefit beekeepers with increases in treatment efficacy, our focus
therefore shifted towards **analysing how we could improve upon the existing
product to meet stakeholder needs**.

## Verdict

Combining the challenges faced by Norroa with stakeholder concerns surrounding
treatment efficacy, practicality, and safety, we responded by adapting our
project design accordingly. **We chose to target genes that killed mites directly
to improve efficacy**, proposed two practical dsRNA delivery systems (inactivated
yeast in pollen patties or engineered living gut bacterium) to prioritise
scalability, and **minimised off-target effects with bioinformatic tools** to
promote safety.

## Evaluate

Evaluating dsRNA against current chemical controls, we identified its three key
benefits: **effectiveness, modularity, and biodegradability**. Its new mode of
action could kill mites resistant to conventional chemical acaricides and be
incorporated into IPM, its modularity enables dsRNA resistance to potentially be
managed by targeting other genes, and it would naturally be degraded in the
ecosystem by UV light and microbial systems, further reducing risks to
non-target organisms. Ultimately, **we decided to proceed with our RNAi-based
approach as opposed to other chemicals**, since it was feasible from regulatory
and scientific perspectives, could better support beekeepers, and was expected
to have less environmental impacts. Reflecting on what we heard and observed, we
changed the specifics of our approach, modifying the way we selected our gene
target to improve efficacy and safety, and inspiring us to propose delivery
methods with enhanced commercial scalability. As we added more and more features,
we realised **we could potentially develop NECTAR into a platform for designing
RNAi-based treatments**.
